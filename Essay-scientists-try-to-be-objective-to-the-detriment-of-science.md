I believe that scientists, in their pursuit of objectivity, attempt to eliminate subjectivity from scientific practice, occasionally to the detriment of scientific progress. 

Consider, for example, the role of beauty in science. Beauty has historically served as a criterion for theory assessment. Before the nineteenth century, beauty was often associated with divine influence and perfection. This can be traced back to Plato, whose conception of the heavens as perfect and harmonious influenced the preference for circular planetary orbits. Circular motion was considered simple, elegant, and perfect, and consequently, planetary motion was expected to conform to it. This preference persisted throughout much of the history of astronomy, even as increasingly elaborate models involving epicycles were developed to reconcile circular motion with astronomical observations. It was eventually challenged by Kepler's elliptical orbits, which demonstrated that planetary motion did not conform to this long-standing expectation of circular perfection. 

Yet beauty has not disappeared as a criterion for scientific work. Rather, its meaning has evolved. In contemporary physics, beauty is increasingly associated with mathematical properties such as simplicity, symmetry, and generality. [[mathematical-beauty|Hossenfelder-how-beauty-leads-physics-astray]] explores this through physicists dissatisfaction with the standard model of particle physics because it is perceived as mathematically inelegant. In response, theories such as supersymmetry and string theory gained attraction, in part because they promise a more elegant and mathematically unified description of nature.

But, when beauty becomes an expectation that physical theories must satisfy, there is a risk that scientists begin favouring theories for how they ought to describe the universe rather than for what experimental evidence tells us about how the universe actually behaves. This can become detrimental to scientific progress when we fail to acknowledge the subjective values underlying these preferences.

Physicists and philosophers have attempted to provide a mathematical or otherwise objective definitions of beauty. But perhaps, in doing so, they risk overlooking the subjectivity that remains inherent in the concept. We still lack a consensus on what makes a theory beautiful, and what one scientist considers elegant may not be particularly compelling to another. Rather than attempting to eliminate this subjectivity by giving beauty an objective definition, it might be more productive for scientists to acknowledge that subjectivity will always play some role in scientific progress.

Science is, after all, a fundamentally human endeavor, and it will inevitably reflect aspects of our humanity. What matters, then, is recognizing where and how our values influence scientific practice. When a physicist chooses to pursue a particular theory because it is mathematically elegant or aesthetically appealing, they should be conscious of the reasons motivating that choice. There is nothing inherently wrong with following an aesthetically appealing theory or exploring where its mathematical elegance might lead. The problem arises when beauty becomes an unquestioned standard, potentially directing decades of research towards a theory that is difficult to test, while other possibilities receive comparatively little attention. Acknowledging the role of beauty allows scientists to remain critical of their own motivations and to recognize when an aesthetic preference might be leading them down an unproductive path.

As Hossenfelder writes:

> "The more difficult new experiments become, the more care scientists must take to not sleepwalk into a dead end while caught up in a beautiful dream" (p. 35).

This also brings to mind [[Douglas-2007-rejecting-value-free-science]]. Douglas argues that the value-free ideal is both unattainable and undesirable. Importantly, pretending that science is value-free does not eliminate the influence of values; it merely makes them less visible. When values are not explicitly acknowledged, they can continue to influence scientific practice subconsciously, without being subjected to the scrutiny they deserve. 

I think the same concern applies to the role of beauty in physics. Attempting to establish beauty as an objective criterion for theory assessment does not necessarily remove the subjective preferences underlying it. Instead, it risks disguising those preferences as objective scientific standards. Perhaps scientific objectivity is better served not by attempting to eliminate values, but by making their influence explicit, allowing them to be critically examined and challenged.
