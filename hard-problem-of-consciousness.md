David Chalmers introduced the idea of the **hard problem of consciousness** = the challenge of explaining *why* we have subjective, first-person experiences (qualia)

**easy problem of consciousness** =
- how does the brain discriminate information?
- how does it integrate information?
- how does it control behavior?
- how does it report mental states?

**hard problem of consciousness** =
- why are these processes accompanied by experience

Note this is not a distinction between easy and hard scientific problems; it is a distinction between different kinds of explanatory questions.

A different way of looking at the hard problem of consciousness is in light of Joseph Levine's **explanatory gap**[^1]. These are distinct views but both explore the idea of why qualia introduces a problem for the scientific study of consciousness.

*Why is there something it is like to be conscious? Why do physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience?*  = What kinds of explanatory reasons could we give for why there seems to be consciousness at all?

These are not the same questions posed by [[Nagel-what-is-it-like-to-be]] and [[Jackson-what-Mary-didn't-know]] that there doesn't seem to be any causal theory that would allow us to reduce or identity qualia. Even if there were, there would still be an explanatory problem of why there's consciousness at all.

Consider Pain = C-fibre firing. Even if the identity is true, Levine argues that something remains unexplained. We can understand what C-fibre firing does, how C-fibre firing is physically realized, how it causes behaviour, etc. but there is still the question of: why does C-fibre firing feel like pain?

Note that we've defined [[phenomenology|phenomenal-consciousness]] as *epiphenomenal* (it is causally inert). If there is no causal role, then why do we experience pain? 

**Identity doesn't entail explanation.** Levine's question is "would establishing the identity explain why the experience has phenomenal character it does?" If we were to find a theoretical reduction that shows that our conscious experience is part of the world such that we are no longer missing out any facts (Nagel-Jackson argument), it could still be explanatorily incomplete. An identity can be true without immediately providing an intuitive explanation of the phenomena.

Two ways to think about this problem:
- **Explanatory gap** = physical account of the world offers no explanation to why consciousness exists at all (or in the way that it does) 
- **explanatory leftovers**[^2] = stuff left over (qualia) that the physical account of the world cannot explain as to why they exist 




[^1]: "Materialism and qualia: the explanatory gap" (1983)
[^2]: Nick Ray's terminology
