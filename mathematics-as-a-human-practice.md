When we look at mathematics, we are looking at this monolithic field filled with a vast variety of mathematical theorems, proofs, and mathematical entities. Mathematics is a field akin to the other sciences, developing and changing over time.

Penelope Maddy in her paper ([[Maddy-2008-How-applied-maths-became-pure]]) showcases how "the historical rise of pure mathematics has coincided with the gradual shift in our understanding of how mathematics works in application to the world".

In Plato's time, Mathematics began as the highest form knowledge, the study of eternal and unchanging abstract Forms, while science was the uncertain and changeable opinion about the world. The scientific revolution brought about the beginnings of experimental natural science as we know today. In the famous words of Galileo, 
> "Nature is written in that great book . . in the mathematical language, and the symbols are triangles, circles and other geometrical figures, without whose help it is impossible to comprehend a single word of it; without which one wanders in vain through a dark labyrinth". 

The universe operates according to mathematical laws, which we uncover by bringing mathematics to bear on our observations. Mathematics and science were one of the same. Mathematics was the language best used to describe the world.

As described by Kline:
> ". . . Descartes, Newton, Euler, and many others believed mathematics to be the accurate description of real phenomena . . . they regarded their work as the uncovering of the mathematical design of the universe . . . gradually and unwittingly mathematicians began to introduce concepts that had little or no direct physical meaning."

Over the course of the 19th century, mathematics began to progress beyond concepts suggested by experience following the pursuit of various purely mathematical goals: negative numbers, complex numbers, n-dimensional spaces, etc.
