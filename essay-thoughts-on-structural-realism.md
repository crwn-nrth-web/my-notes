An important question for scientists to ask themselves is whether or not our scientific theories aim to provide an accurate description of the world. This has historically been an extensive debate, developing two main arguments on either side. The no miracles argument argues that the empirical successfulness of our present theories must mean that they have latched onto the blueprint of the universe, rather than it being lucky guesses or a miracle. On the other hand, the pessimistic meta induction argument asks what rational basis do we have for believing current theories describe the world, given that past successful theories were abandoned and are now regarded as false. 

Worrall offers structural realism as the "the best of both worlds", a defensible form of realism while still conceding to the argument from scientific revolutions. Moreover, this form of realism offers an insight into why mathematics works so well in physics.

Pessimistic meta induction compels the realist to ask whether the history of science speaks in favor of successive scientific theories being increasingly good "approximations" to the truth. Worrall notes that realism is plausible only if science has been "essentially cumulative", i.e. deposed theories themselves (not just their successful empirical consequences) have lived on in some way. He hence argues for structural realism: what survives theory change are the relational structures, rather than the claims about what the underlying entities are. 

When Fresnel’s elastic-solid ether theory of light was replaced by Maxwell’s electromagnetism, the picture of light as vibrations in a mechanical ether was abandoned, but Fresnel’s equations for the relative intensities of reflected and refracted light carried over intact. Worrall argues that this shows Fresnel’s theory got the structure right: the quantities in the equations were reinterpreted as electric and magnetic field strengths, while the mathematical relations among them stayed the same. Worrall himself concedes that this case is not the best representation, since the equations are preserved exactly. More commonly, old equations reappear only as limiting cases of the new theory (e.g. relativistic mechanics tend towards Newtonian mechanics when v/c approaches to 0). This still supports structural realism, because the realist claims approximate truth, not exact truth. Old equations arising as limiting cases showcase how they were approximately correct, with the new theory explaining why and where they fail. In other words, the best explanation of the old equations success is they had approximately captured real structures. 

This continuity is what the no-miracles argument needs: the old theories were successful because they latched onto real relations, even while misdescribing the relata. I find this a compelling compromise. It concedes what the history of science forces us to concede, while keeping realism rather than retreating to instrumentalism.

It is important to note the distinction between ontic and epistemic structural realism. Worrall's version is epistemic. The world may contain objects with intrinsic natures, but all we can know are their structural relations. Conversely, ontic structural realism holds that structure is all there is. The epistemic reading is more modest and, I think, better supported: it takes the physics at face value without converting a limit on what theories tell us into a claim about what exists.

---

Much of modern theoretical physics also seems to be moving in direction that structural realism describes. In quantum mechanics, identical particles are indistinguishable in a way that classical objects are not, so the theory attends to symmetry of the state rather than the identity of individual particles. What we can say about electrons is how it transforms under certain symmetry groups, what is conserved, and how it interacts. In general relativity, general covariance shows that spacetime points have no existing individuality and are as such relational. Each step towards a more fundamental theory has traded picture of entities with intrinsic properties for a more relational, more mathematical description. 

---

Wigner described the effectiveness of mathematics in physics as unreasonable. But the puzzle presupposes that mathematics and physical content are independent domains which happen to match. Structural realism removes that presupposition. It claims that science describes structures of the world (not objects). Equations and models are what captures the relations (structures) between objects.
