 Wilson begins by pointing out that it has become common to characterize mathematics as "the general science of possible structures". Within mathematics big bag of general "structures", there must be a structure that is structually isomorphic to that of the physical world. 
 
> “I have defined *mathematical optimism* to be the doctrine that every real-life physical structure can be expected to possess a suitable direct representative within the world of mathematics.” (Wilson, 2000, p. 297)

However, there has also been the opinion that only when the processes of nature have a special simplicity can mathematics track it adequately. Wilson calls this =

> "*mathematical opportunism*: it is the job of the applied mathematician to look out for the special circumstances that allow mathematics to say something useful about physical behavior." (Wilson, 2000, p. 297)

###### d'Alembert example

> “the applicability of the classical equation for a vibrating string, which had been developed by d'Alembert. This formula contains a second derivative reflecting the string's change of shape. Intuitively, one expects that a string can be plucked into a "V" configuration. But the equation's reliance upon the second derivative makes it seem as if it can't possibly make sense for a string containing a sharp bend. D'Alembert concluded, in an admirably opportunist spirit, that, although the bent string represents a genuine physical possibility, mathematics cannot expect to treat such a case and must restrict its attention to strings that are regular enough to possess second derivatives. . .
> . . . His reasoning is better parsed as follows: "Claims about infinitesimal relationships as embodied in the wave equation make sense only if they represent limiting distillates of some global regularity displayed in the macroscopic behavior at hand. An infinitesimal claim about tangents, for example, must derive its meaning from the pattern displayed in some regular geometrical figure such as an ellipse or hyperbola. But, there is no reason why nature should need, except by”  (Wilson, 2000, p. 298)

Calculus only works on things that are already smooth everywhere. A tangent at a point on an ellipse or circle makes sense because the entire curve is smooth and regular. The local fact depends on the global pattern. However, nature doesn't have to be smooth, as seen by the fact that the sharp-bent string is perfectly possible in the real world. 

Wilson argues that d'Alembert's point is: math tools comes with built in requirements (smoothness, regularity, etc.) that the real world doesn't always meet. Wilson calls these *"alien elements"* = something that the maths brings along that nature isn't obligated to provide.

This example showcases the two sides:
- opportunism (d'Alembert's view) = since math only works where the world happens to be regular, mathematicians should look for those special cases and stick to them. Math can't handle everything
- optimism (Euler's view) = math can handle everything in principle. Euler realized that a differential equation can build the answer step by step on its own, without needing a pre-existing smooth formula
