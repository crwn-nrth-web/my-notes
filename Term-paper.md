
> Combining structural realism with the mapping account is a choice, not an entailment: one can accept that applications involve structure-preserving mappings while remaining neutral or anti-realist about what the mapped structure is. I argue the combination is nonetheless well motivated. Because suitable mappings are cheap to find, the mapping account needs a constraint on which mappings are appropriate, and the natural candidates are novel predictive success and survival through theory change. These features are puzzling if the mapping is merely a convenient fit. Structural realism explains them: the mappings that persist are those that preserve real relations. The anti-realist can reply that such success is explained by selection or empirical adequacy, but this does not explain why a mapping continues to succeed when the theory’s ontology is replaced. This inference to the best explanation carries the same assumptions as the no-miracles argument, so it persuades those who accept that argument, but for them the combination offers more than either view alone.

> Structural realism needs a source of mathematical structures to map onto the world, and it is tempting to picture this as an atlas of possible structures that exists independently of physics. A naturalist account of mathematics replaces this picture with a historical one. On Kitcher’s view, mathematics is an evolving practice of a community, which changes by standards of its own, such as rigorization, generalization, and the answering of open questions, many of which arise from science. Mathematics is therefore not a finished catalogue waiting for application but a growing one, shaped partly by the problems physics poses. This reduces the apparent miracle that mathematics developed for other purposes later fits physical theory, since the two practices are coupled. But it leaves a question that coupling cannot answer: why the structures that succeed and persist are so stable. That is the role of structural realism. Naturalism explains how mathematics comes to supply mappings, and structural realism explains why the mappings that last deserve trust.

> Coupling explains how mathematics comes to supply structures, but not why the world has regularities that those structures capture so well. Islami’s account addresses this. On her view, modern physics is distinguished by the central role of invariance principles: regularities in events motivate laws, and regularities in those laws are expressed as invariances. Invariance is a structural notion, and mathematics, especially group theory, is built to represent it. A mathematics shaped by physics can therefore fit physical theory because there is stable structure for it to be shaped toward. That the world has such structure is a contingent fact, which my account takes as its empirical premise and does not explain. What it explains is why, given that premise, mathematical mappings succeed and why the ones that persist deserve trust.
