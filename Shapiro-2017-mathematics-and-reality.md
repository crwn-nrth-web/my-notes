> Shapiro, S. (1983). Mathematics and Reality. _Philosophy of Science_, _50_(4), 523–548. http://www.jstor.org/stable/187555
#### Section 1: why does the relationship between mathematics and the non-mathematical world matter

Shapiro defines the [[Philosophy-of-X]] as in part of branch of epistemology: an account of X's goals, methodology and subject matter. He applies this to both the philosophy of science and philosophy of mathematics, and argues that the two are intertwined, since both must address how mathematics relates to knowledge of the world

**Philosophy of science**
Shapiro notes that in most cases, a scientific explanation of a physical event often amount to a mathematical "description". 

>"Many scientific (and engineering) problems are described as tasks of finding the right differential equation, the right formula, the right function, or the right integral to be associated with a class of phenomena." (p. 525)

He describes the goal of science as "to understand the various aspects (e.g. physical, chemical, behavioral) of the world". On first look, (Pythagoreans aside) the world is not mathematical. However, the methodology of science includes considerable mathematics. 

Hence, a philosopher of science must explain 
- "how mathematics can contribute to understanding the prima facie[^1] non-mathematical scientific world." (p. 525) 
- "how and why the mathematical methodology succeeds in predicting and controlling the non-mathematical world." (p. 526).

**Philosophy of mathematics**
Since most mathematics is not aimed directly at the understanding the non-mathematical world, one could argue that it seems possible to account for the goals and practices of mathematics with mentioning the relationship between mathematics and reality (p. 526). But Shapiro points out that this would create a sharp distinction (in kind) between pure and applied mathematics.

A problem with this distinction between pure and applied mathematics is that it is artificial. 
- There is more similarities than differences between the two. "If one examines the practice, writings, and textbooks of both pure and applied mathematics, one finds more similarities than differences concerning such basic aspects as aims, techniques, logic, and even subject matter." (p. 527)
- many branches of applied mathematics (e.g. applied differential equations) are sub-branches of pure mathematics
- applications are occasionally discovered in areas of pure mathematics

Because of this, 
> "a philosophy of mathematics should not only provide an internally coherent account of mathematical goals and practice, it should also account for the importance of mathematics. That is, [it] should explain not only what the mathematician does, but also why he does it, and, in particular, how mathematical knowledge is related to general knowledge." (p. 527)

#### Section 2: traditional philosophies of mathematics

1. **Formalism** = mathematics consists of no more than the manipulation of characters according to rules

The formulas of mathematics are considered to be strings of meaningless characters (not genuine symbols that symbolize something). Also, the rules of the various branches of mathematics are often considered to be arbitrarily chosen. 

E.g. a theorem of arithmetic does not represent a fact about the natural numbers. The theorem is just the result of a series of manipulations according to the rules of arithmetic
