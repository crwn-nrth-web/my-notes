**to quine** = to deny resolutely the existence of something real or significant

Dennett grants that conscious experience has properties, and each person's states of consciousness have properties that have the experiential content. However, he argues that conscious experience has no properties that are special in any of the ways qualia is supposed to be special.

Dennett's argument is based on defining what qualia even is, that even the source concept (the pre-theoretical notion) is so thoroughly confused that it would be better, tactically, to declare that there are simply no qualia.

He does this by outlining 15 *intuition pumps* designed to flush out offending intuitions regarding qualia.

#### arguments against the special properties of qualia

1. **watching you eat cauliflower**

*intuition* = seeing someone eat something you absolutely hate (e.g. cauliflower) leads to plausible hypothesis that cauliflower must taste different to them. This makes sense because food often tastes different to ourselves at different time. So, surely we can talk whether "the way juice tastes to me at time $t$" is the same or different from "the way juice tastes to me at time $t'$" or "the way juice tastes to Jones at time t"

*problem with the intuition* = it presumes we can isolate the qualia from everything else that is going on. It mistakes that there is such residual property (the way things look, sound, feel, taste, smell to various individuals at various times, independently of how they are subsequently disposed to behave or believe)  to take seriously
