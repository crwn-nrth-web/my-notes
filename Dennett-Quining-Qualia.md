**to quine** = to deny resolutely the existence of something real or significant

Dennett grants that conscious experience has properties, and each person's states of consciousness have properties that have the experiential content. However, he argues that conscious experience has no properties that are special in any of the ways qualia is supposed to be special.

Dennett's argument is based on defining what qualia even is, that even the source concept (the pre-theoretical notion) is so thoroughly confused that it would be better, tactically, to declare that there are simply no qualia.

He does this by outlining 15 *intuition pumps* designed to flush out offending intuitions regarding qualia.

#### arguments against the special properties of qualia

Traditionally, qualia are supposed to be properties of a subject's mental states that are (p. 385) =
1. ineffable
2. intrinsic
3. private
4. directly or immediately apprehensible in conscious

This can be seen by the following "intuition pumps" =

1. **watching you eat cauliflower**

*intuition* = seeing someone eat something you absolutely hate (e.g. cauliflower) leads to plausible hypothesis that cauliflower must taste different to them. This makes sense because food often tastes different to ourselves at different time. So, surely we can talk whether "the way juice tastes to me at time $t$" is the same or different from "the way juice tastes to me at time $t'$" or "the way juice tastes to Jones at time t"

*problem with the intuition* = it presumes we can isolate the qualia from everything else that is going on. It mistakes that there is such residual property (the way things look, sound, feel, taste, smell to various individuals at various times, independently of how they are subsequently disposed to behave or believe)  to take seriously

2. **the wine tasting machine**

*intuition* = could human wine tasters be replaced with a machine? If we were to program a transducer that could replace taste buds and olfactory organs. The machine might perform better than humans on reasonable tests of accuracy and consistency, but surely, the system will never have, and enjoy, what we do when we taste a wine, i.e. the qualia of conscious experience. “Whatever informational, dispositional, functional properties its internal states have, none of them will be special in the way qualia are.”

3. **the inverted spectrum**

*intuition* = "how do i know that you and I see the same subjective colour when we look at something? Since we both learned colour words by being shown public coloured objects, our verbal behaviour will match even if we experience entirely different subjective colours”

4. **the brainstorm machine**

*intuition* = suppose that there was a neuroscientific apparatus that feeds your visual experience into someone else's brain. With eyes closed, the person accurately reports everything you are looking at but with inverted qualia (sky is yellow, grass red, etc.). When the technician inverts the connecting cable by 180 degrees, now the person reports the sky to be blue, grass green, etc. What would be the right orientation of the cable? Designing such a machine would mean calibration by the normalization of two subjects' reports, which is difficult (or not possible) to do. This shows that there is no intersubjective comparison of qualia possible.

5. **neurosurgical prank**

*intuition* = here we are comparing experiences all in one mind. Suppose you wake up one morning and see that the grass has turned red, the sky yellow, etc. No one else notices any colour anomalies, so the problem must be with you. You are entitled to conclude that you have undergone visual colour qualia inversion. 

This makes it seem that qualia are acceptable properties after all, since propositions about hem can be justifiably asserted, empirically verified and even explained. But when combined with intuition pump 6, the problem emerges

6. **alternative neurosurgery**

*intuition* = there are at least two ways in which the inversion effect described in (5) can be created:
1. invert one of the early qualia-producing channels (e.g. in the optical nerve). This inverts the qualia
2. invert certain memory-access links (whatever accomplishes the tacit comparison of today's hue to past ones). This does not invert the qualia, just your memory-anchored dispositions to them

Since two different ways can produce the same introspective effects (with only one operation actually inverting the qualia) then nothing in the subject's experience can favor one hypotheses over the other. Hence, the state of the person's own qualia must be unknowable to him as the state of anyone else's qualia. There is no longer privileged access or direct apprehension that qualia is assumed to have.

> “If there are qualia, they are even less accessible to our ken than we had thought. Not only are the classical intersubjective comparisons impossible (as (6) the Brainstorm machine shows), but we cannot tell in our own cases whether our qualia have been inverted -- at least not by introspection.” (P. 389)

#### arguments about mistaking qualia

The following intuition pumps explores the idea that people might be mistaken about their own qualia

7. **Chase and Sanborn**

*intuition* = suppose there are two coffee-tasters who worked for Maxwell coffee House. After 6 years:
> "I hate to admit it, but I'm not enjoying this work anymore. When I came to Maxwell House six years ago, I thought Maxwell House coffee was the best-tasting coffee in the world. I was proud to have a share in the responsibility for preserving that flavor over the years. And we've done our job well; the coffee tastes just the same today as it tasted when I arrived. But, you know, I no longer like it! My tastes have changed. I’ve become a more sophisticated coffee drinker. I no longer like that taste at all." -- Chase

possibilities =
1. Chase's coffee-taste-qualia have stayed constant, while his reactive attitudes to those qualia, devolving on his canons of aesthetic judgment, etc., have shifted, which is what he seems, in his informal, casual way, to be asserting.
2. Chase is simply wrong about the constancy of his qualia; they have shifted gradually and imperceptibly over the years, while his standards of taste haven't budged—in spite of his delusions about having become more sophisticated. He is in the state Sanborn claims to be in, but just lacks Sanborn's self-knowledge.
3. Chase is in some state between (1) and (2); his qualia have shifted some and his standards of judgment have also slipped.

>"When I arrived here, shortly before you did, I, like you, thought Maxwell House coffee was tops in flavor. And now I, like you, really don't care for the coffee we're making. But my tastes haven't changed; my tasters have changed. That is, I think something has gone wrong with my taste buds or some other part of my taste-analyzing perceptual machinery. Maxwell House coffee doesn't taste to me the way it used to taste; if only it did, I'd still love it, for I still think that taste is the best taste in coffee. Now I'm not saying we haven't done our job well. You other tasters all agree that the taste is the same, and I must admit that on a day-to-day basis I can detect no change either. So it must be my problem alone. I guess I'm no longer cut out for this work." -- Sanborn

possibilities =
1. Sanborn is right; his qualia have shifted, due to some sort of derangement in his perceptual machinery, but his standards have indeed remained constant.
2. Sanborn's standards have shifted unbeknownst to him. He is thus misremembering his past experiences, in what we might call a *nostalgia effect*. Sanborn's nostalgia-tinged memory of good old Maxwell House is similarly distorted. 
	- Think of the familiar experience of returning to some object from your childhood (a classroom desk, a tree-house) and finding it much smaller than you remember it to have been. Presumably as you grew larger your internal standard for what was large grew with you somehow, but your memories (which are stored as fractions or multiples of that standard) didn't compensate, and hence when you consult your memory, it returns a distorted judgment. 
3. Sanborn is in some state between (1) and (2)
