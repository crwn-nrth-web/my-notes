The paper addresses two main questions:
- are there genuine mathematical explanations of physical phenomena?
- how can we understand mathematical explanations of physical phenomena?

It also addresses how mapping accounts like [[Pincock-2004-New-perspective-on-the-problem-of-applying-mathematics|Pincock-mapping-account]] and [[Bueno-&-Colyvan-2011-inferential-account-of-applied-maths|Bueno-&-Colyvan-inferential-account]] fail to deal with the use of idealization in physical theories. 

The disagreement Batterman has with the mapping accounts is that these focus on "static relationships" between mathematical models and the world. Instead, by taking limits, you could more accurately represent, for example, infinites. 

#### Types of mathematical explanations

**on entities** =
> “If mathematical objects are abstract and acausal, then, if explanation in the natural sciences is fundamentally causal, mathematical entities cannot play an explanatory role. This objection is only as strong as the idea that all explanations in physical theory are causal explanations. But, it seems to me that there are very good reasons to deny that all physical explanations are causal explanations.” (p. 2)

**on operations** =
> “there are explanations which, while mathematical, do not make reference to such objects. Rather, these explanations appeal to (or better 'involve') mathematical operations.” (Batterman, 2010, p. 4)
- An example of operation is the role of "limits"

> “taking the thermodynamic limit is an explanatory essential mathematical operation, then this is a case in which, while we have a genuine mathematical explanation of physical phenomenon, there is no appeal to the existence of mathematical entities or their properties.” (Batterman, 2010, p. 7)

#### Idealizations

Batterman is strong on his opinion of idealizations that they are "necessarily false" and that "nothing in the physical world actually corresponds to the idealization".

**Galilean understanding of idealization** (McMullian, 1985) = “This is a view in which idealizations are perfectly respectable in science, provided that one can (in principle) tell a story about how they might be removed through further work.” (Batterman, 2010, p. 16)

The Galilean understanding of idealization explains why despite being false it plays an explanatory role, i.e. the falsehood is epistemically valuable

This places mathematics as a practice, focusing on the trajectories of theories through idealizations

**non-Galilean (non-traditional) idealizations** = "very idea of such a de-idealizing story is seriously problematic, if not incompatible with the explanatory features of the model. These nontraditional idealizations play essential explanatory roles involving operations or mathematical processes without representing the system(s) in question.” (Batterman, 2010, p. 23)

Non-Galilean idealizations are characterized by *singular limits*  which makes it hard to smoothly de-idealize = “A singular limit is one in which the behavior as one approaches the limit is qualitatively different from the behavior one would have at the limit.” (Batterman, 2010, p. 17)

| Galilean idealization                                                                                                                                                                            | Non-Galilean idealization                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| (in principle) eliminable or de-idealizble                                                                                                                                                       | explanatorily essential and non-eliminable idealization                                                                                                        |
| you can start with an idealized simplified model and can (in principle) de-idealize by adding back in the omitted real-world variables (such as air resistance or friction) to increase accuracy | in thermodynamics, treating matter in ways known to be false (like infinite limits) is the only way the math yields an explanation (it cannot be de-idealized) |

#### Batterman on regularities

Most (not all) explanations in physics and mathematics are explanations of patterns or regularities. 

> “In order to explain the robustness (the repeatability) of the patterns we see, our mathematical representations have to be stable under changes of all these different details. One important way mathematics allows us to do this is through the taking of limits. Limits are a means by which various details can be thrown away.” (Batterman, 2010, p. 20)

> “We need to understand why we have these regularities and invariances. *We need, that is, to ask for an explanation of those very regularities and invariances.* This is the fundamental explanatory question . . . *To explain and understand the robustness of patterns and regularities, one sometimes needs to focus on places where those very regularities break down.* The existence of patterns or regularities in the world, and our desire to understand and explain them, has led us rather straightforwardly to investigate singularities in mathematical limiting operations.” (Batterman, 2010, p. 21)
