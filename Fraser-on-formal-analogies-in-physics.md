 >Fraser, Draft Chapter 7: Explaining the Applicability of mathematics, Formal Analogies in Modern Physics
 
The central question of this chapter is "what does the fact that it is possible to apply mathematics in the ways in which it is actually applied in physics reveal about the nature of physics?"

This chapter argues that "[Fraser] account of purely formal analogies is compatible with a naturalistically acceptable explanation of the applicability of mathematics."

#### Wigner and Steiner

Fraser begins by reconstructing a line of argument from [[Wigner-and-Islami-on-the-unreasonable-effectiveness-of-mathematics-in-physics|wigner-on-the-unreasonable-effectiveness-of-mathematics-in-physics]] (p. 3 - 5)

Taking Wigner's conclusion to be "the miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift we neither understand nor deserve" (Wigner, p. 237) where the applicability of mathematics being miraculous means that there is no rational explanation for it.

His argument for this conclusion lies on his *empirical law of epistemology* (the appropriate and accuracy of the mathematical formulation of the laws of nature). Wigner's miracle is that the empirical law of epistemology, accepted as a matter of faith, has turned out to be correct. 

Fraser outlines two options for criticizing Wigner's argument in order to avoid the skeptical conclusion that the applicability of mathematics is miraculous (p. 5)
1. an option open to anti-realist is to reject the scientific realist assumption. This can be done by critiquing Wigner's [[no-miracles-argument]] for mathematics being the correct language, rejecting the reasoning of the argument on the basis that it rests on an [[Foster-proposed-solution-to-the-problem-of-induction|inference-to-the-best-explanation]] 
2. claiming that mathematics is not the correct language, as it does not yield absolutely correct predictions. Instead, mathematics could be the best language we have, but not the correct one. This, however, could still lead it being miraculous for being so close to the correct language.

#### Mark Wilson on applied mathematics

[[Wilson-2017-Physics-Avoidance]] = “nature rarely arranges its affairs for our calculational convenience but forever forces us into seeking clever work-arounds for improving our computational lot in life” (Wilson, p. 364)
- the computational task involved in extracting "usable data" from a set of differential equations can be difficult
- physics avoidance is used when an evolutionary problem becomes too difficult to solve, it can help to switch to a related equilibrium problem that is more tractable

> “There are two relevant themes of [Mark Wilson]’s account of applied mathematics: hard mathematical problems can be difficult due to both particular features of a specified context of application and general computational obstacles. An example of the former is what [Wilson] calls the “unreasonable cooperativeness” of applied mathematics: even after we implement the physics avoidance strategy of shifting from an evolutionary to an equilibrium modeling problem, we may encounter further difficulties. (Fraser, p. 13 - 14)

##### Wilson on Naturalism

> "[Wilson]’s naturalism is based on a loose, “quasi-biological” analogy between frogs’ strategies for fly catching and humans’ mathematical strategies for solving computational problems. In the course of setting out this account of naturalism, [Wilson] also sketches an account of applied mathematics. From an applied mathematics perspective, physics avoidance involves finding effective mathematical strategies for improving our computational abilities when we run up against a recalcitrant world." (p. 14)

For a frog, the strategy of waiting to catch a fly until it loops around in its flight path is evolutionarily advantageous because it is easier to predict the future flight path at a loop. In a similar way, humans are more swiftly adaptive to varying circumstances because they can "readily borrow and retune reasoning stratagems developed for task A to become novel routines for achieving task B, despite the fact that the relevant subject matters scarcely resemble one another beyond" ([[Wilson-2017-Physics-Avoidance|Wilson (2017)]], p. 384)

##### Wilson on Wigner

In [[Wilson-2000-the-unreasonable-uncooperativeness-of-mathematics|Wilson (2000)]], he critiques the view he labels as *lazy optimism* (there is a neat fit between mathematical representations and the world) that fits with Wigner's view that mathematics is the correct language. Wilson argue that lazy optimism as unwarranted and offers *honest optimism* and *opportunism* as alternatives. 

To characterize honest optimism and opportunism, Fraser uses the following example. Consider modelling a goose's flight path in a plane under specified wind conditions. According to Wilson, three levels need to be distinguished: 
1. goose's actual flight curve
2. the differential equation's true solution modelling of the flight
3. the numerical estimates we can actually obtain from numerical reasoning rules (e.g. Euler's method)

(2) has a "mildly transcendental" relationship with (3) because (3) approaches (2) but in practice can never coincide with (2) because of the infinitely many "computational refinements" that would be required. 

> “Honest mathematical optimists maintain that there is good reason (derived from knowledge of level (3) and empirical data) to believe that there is some level (2) solution that accurately represents any level (1) actual flight path of the goose. Mathematical opportunists believe that mathematical models that culminate in level (3) computed approximations is only applicable in special circumstances.” (Fraser, p. 15)

There is also *semantic mimicry* which puts pressure on the the [[no-miracles-argument]] connection between predictive success and accurate description of the world. 

*Semantic mimicry* is when (3) appear to correspond to (2) when in fact there is some different and more complicated (2*) that better represents (1), the target in the world. 

## Using formal analogies in physical theories

Abstraction = freedom to attach different physical interpretations to mathematically similar elements of representation

Generality = having a broad scope of application

Bartha's [^1] version of Steiner's argument is that the following three propositions are incompatible (p. 6) =
1. purely formal analogies have played a crucial role in many fundamental discoveries
2. purely formal analogies contribute nothing to the plausibility of a physical hypothesis
	(a) purely formal analogies are based on mathematical similarities with no physical significance
	 (b) reasoning about the physical world based on purely mathematical similarities contributes nothing to the plausibility of a hypothesis
3. the fundemental discoveries were in fact based on (somewhat) plausible arguments

Fraser rejects the proposition (2).

> “The hypothesis that is supported by a purely formal analogy is that a solution strategy for an applied mathematical problem that was successfully applied within a mathematical representation for domain A can be transferred to a mathematical representation for domain B to solve a mathematically similar problem. The existence of the purely formal analogy does contribute to the plausibility of this hypothesis in (at least) the minimal sense that it is worth the effort to try applying this solution strategy to the mathematical representation for domain B.” (Fraser, p. 18)


Fraser's account is naturalistic in nature = 
> “Applying mathematics does have an important human dimension because it reflects a human capacity for solving problems. Human limitations and preferences (e.g., for beauty and convenience) may be factors.” (Fraser, p. 18)

[^1]: Bartha, P. F. (2010). By Parallel Reasoning: The Construction and Evaluation of Analogical Arguments. Oxford: Oxford University Press.
