> Wilson, P. A. (1993). The Anthropic Principle. In N. S. Hetherington (Ed.), _Cosmology: Historical, Literary, Philosophical, Religious and Scientific Perspectives_ (pp. 505–513). Garland Publishing.
 
**Anthropic Principle** = "life in some way constrains the fundamental features of the universe." 

> "The anthropic principle hints at the existence of deep connections between the universe and humanity, makes striking claims about mankind's place in the universe, and evokes classical arguments for a divine cosmic designer. The principle involves additional philosophical and religious themes as well: the significance and value of human life, the goal-directedness of nature, and the degree to which the universe is anthropocentric." (Thinking about the universe, p. 502)

The universe is just barely fit for for human survival. If several of its basics physical features had been even slightly different, human life could not have occurred. The anthropic principle is an attempt to account for the fact that the universe seems "finely-tuned" for humans.

Brandon Carter (1974)[^1] points out a **logical constraint** on the observation of the universe by intelligent beings: of course, the universe seems to be "finely-tuned" for humans, all properties of the universe must be consistent with the presence of intelligent beings, given that such beings are around to study the properties. "Intelligent beings cannot (obviously) find themselves in spatial or temporal regions of the universe that are uninhabitable by intelligent beings" (The Anthropic Principle, p. 507)

The logical constraint needs to be taken into account when assessing coincidences, especially in regards to the apparent fine-tuning of the universe. 

> "For example, the remarkableness of the large-scale homogeneity of the universe is reduced once we realize that humans could not exist if the universe were much different. Given that we exist, and that large-scale homogeneity of the universe is a necessary condition for our existence, then the universe is guaranteed, in a logical sense, to be homogeneous." (p. 508)

The logical form of the anthropic principle is not at all *anthropocentric*. Given that the logical form applies equally to the necessary conditions of all living things (for any X it is true that, given the existence of X, the necessary conditions for X must be observed), then there is no reason to assume that the principle should place special emphasis on humankind.

A **teleological** form of the anthropic principle[^2] states that the universe is habitable precisely *so that* intelligent life can evolve in it. Habitability is the goal or end of the universe, so the universe must be habitable.



















[^1]: in this article, Carter coined the term "anthropic principle"
[^2]: in Barrow, J.D, and Tipler, F.J (1986), The Anthropic Cosmological Principle
