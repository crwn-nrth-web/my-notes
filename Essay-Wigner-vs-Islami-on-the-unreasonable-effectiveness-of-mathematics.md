> This paper arose after an in-class discussion on Wigner and Islami where folks got hung up on the fact that Islami's paper was written years after Wigner, and yet she claims that her arguments (which seemed contradictory to Wigner's thesis) are what Wigner really meant. So, this paper focused on the idea that while Islami's reconstruction of Wigner is questionable, her own account does have genuine explanatory value

Wigner's paper *The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics* tends to make bold statements that have sparked many interpretations and rebuttals. One interesting "interpretation" is offered by Islami in her paper *A match not made in heaven*. Not only does it offer a different understanding of Wigner than the common literal reading, but her reconstruction seems to lead to a significantly different conclusion about why mathematics is effective in physics. Nevertheless, I believe that if we separate Islami's argument from the question of whether it accurately represents what Wigner meant, her argument has merit.

To begin, the common literal understanding of Wigner's argument is as follows: Mathematics consists of abstract human-invented concepts that are developed largely independently of their applicability to the physical world. Mathematicians are  instead more concerned with mathematical qualities, such as manipulability, formal beauty, generality, etc. It is therefore surprising (or as Wigner boldly calls "unreasonable", "irrational", "mysterious", "a miracle") that physics is able to take these mathematical concepts and fit them perfectly into the descriptions of the natural world (i.e. our scientific theories). The surprising feature for Wigner is not just that physics uses mathematics, but that mathematics developed for mathematical internal reasons should turn out to be so effective in describing physical reality. This is what gives Wigner' argument its sense of mystery and what leads him to describe the effectiveness of mathematics as unreasonable.

Islami, on the other hand, offers what she calls a "reconstruction of Wigner's main thesis" (Islami, 2017, p. 3). In her interpretation, she argues that Wigner is responding to a more particular and limited question than otherwise assumed: "what is it about modern (theoretical) physics that makes mathematics an appropriate language for the formulation of its laws?" (p. 3). Islami further argues that understanding Wigner's paper in the wider context of his work offers a different conclusion to the Applicability question. She distinguishes between two questions within Wigner's applicability question: the Why question, "why is mathematics useful in physics?", and the How question, "how is mathematics useful in physics?" (p.7). Her approach to the why question is to investigate the How: that the particular structure of modern physics, particularly its reliance on invariance principles, allows it to be mathematically formulated. Mathematics is effective in its application to physics because the laws of physics have the structure that mathematics is good at representing. 

For Islami, the distinguishing feature of modern physics is the central role of invariance principles. These principles give physical laws a universal structure: the laws remain the same under specified transformations of physical systems or frames of reference. The fact there are regularities in events motivate the formulation for laws of nature, and regularities in those laws lead to invariance principles which mathematics is particularly well suited to representing. In this sense, mathematics is effective in physics not simply because mathematical concepts happen to correspond to physical reality, but because the structure of modern physical laws is one that mathematical structures are capable of expressing. If there were no regularities in nature, mathematics would not have been as successful in the formulation of our scientific theories.

Now, there is an important interpretative difficulty with Islami's reconstruction. Islami points out that "there is convincing reasons to think that the conclusion of this [Wigner's] paper is that the effectiveness of mathematics is unreasonable" (p. 4) and Wigner himself uses bold explicit language to say so. Nevertheless, Islami argues for a reconstruction of Wigner's thesis that supports a less mysterious account in which the structure of modern physics explains why mathematics is appropriate for formulating its laws. It is reasonable to question whether Islami's reconstruction accurately captures Wigner's conclusion. However, whether Islami's paper is a historically accurate reconstruction of Wigner's position is a separate question from whether the account itself is philosophically successful.

Treating Islami's argument as an independent response to the applicability problem, I find her argument to be philosophically valuable. Her argument identifies a concrete reason why mathematics is useful to physics, removing an aspect of the applicability of mathematics that could be considered mysterious. Of course, this does not necessarily eliminate every aspect of Wigner's original puzzle. Even if Islami explains why mathematics is well suited to expressing the laws of modern physics, one can still ask why mathematics, often developed for reasons internal to mathematics itself, happened to develop the structures that physics would later find so useful. Nevertheless, I do not think this residual question undermines Islami's argument. Her account makes an important contribution by showing that the usefulness of mathematics in physics can be understood, at least in part, by examining the structure of physics itself. Whether or not this is ultimately what Wigner intended, Islami provides a philosophically valuable explanation of why mathematics is such an appropriate language for modern theoretical physics.
