Pincock begins by giving a great analogy for the **applicability problem** = 

> If we are looking to launch a satellite, we begin by "taking the specifications of the latest rocket engine and consider what velocity and height the rocket will reach before burnout. But the velocity and altitude that will be reached depends on the mass of the rocket, which decreases as it uses its fuel . . . we derive equations for the magnitudes that we are interested in and solve them. The equations are derived from basic equations of our scientific theory, along with certain assumptions like the conservation of linear momentum. Given our knowledge of mathematics we can solve these equations using the appropriate constants and we conclude that at burnout the rocket will be 100 km above the Earth and that it will have a velocity of 2.16 x 103 m/s away from the Earth. . . 
> 
> . . . I want to draw your attention towards in this somewhat fanciful story is the natural way in which mathematics entered the picture . . . when we were scientists, we began by wondering about rockets, satellites, and the Earth, and we came up with equations that govern rockets, satellites, and the Earth and solved these equations using mathematics. ==🟠What is unsettling here is that our mathematical theories seem to be about things like numbers, functions, and operations like addition and multiplication. But why should a theory about these sorts of things be relevant to a rocket?== It is as if we started talking about tables and chairs, and then moved to considering koalas and Eucalyptus trees, and finally returned to some verdict about tables and chairs. Mathematics seems to have a subject matter that is distinct from the physical world. ==🟠The problem, then, is to say what connection there is between the physical world and mathematics that can explain the successful application of mathematics in scientific reasoning.== (p. 136 - 137)

Pincock's approach to answering the applicability problem is to begin by focusing on the content or meaning of the statements we use, i.e. determining what mixed statements means 

**mixed statements** = the occurrence of what seems to be mathematical terms along with non-mathematical terms
- e.g. "the mass of the satellite is 100 kg", "the gravitational acceleration is 9.81 $m / s^2$"

In this article, Pincock is considering accounts for the application of mathematics "in a way that is independent of accounts of pure mathematics"
### relation vs no-relation accounts

A division of accounts of application can be made based on whether or not an account accepts mixed statements as claims about complex relations between mathematical and non-mathematical objects

**no-relation account** denies that that any relation connects mathematical and physical domains when mathematics is applied

**Relation account** accepts that mixed statements depend on a relation between mathematical and physical domains for their truth

*Internal relation account* 
- requires that an internal relation obtain between the mathematical domain and the physical world
- an internal relation for an object $a$ is a relation that $a$ must stand in if it is to be that particular object

*external relation account*
- mixed statements require for their truth the existence of an external relation between the mathematical and physical domain
- external relation is any relation that does not involve the criteria of identity of the mathematical objects

One external relation approach is the *structuralist approach*
- the truth of mixed statements depend upon the existence of mappings that have the right properties, and these properties that can be picked out in structural terms
- this structuralist approach is independent of the structuralist account of pure mathematics
