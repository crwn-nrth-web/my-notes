Levine takes Kripke's metaphysical argument against materialism and transforms it into an epistemological one. He argues that 

> “psycho-physical identity statements leave a significant explanatory gap, and, as a corollary, that we don’t have any way of determining exactly which psycho-physical identity statements are true.” (Levine, 1983, p. 1)

*psycho-physical statements* = statements that describes the systematic relationship between physical stimuli and psychological experiences. They bridge the gap between the physical world and the subjective mental world

#### Kripke's metaphysical argument

Take the materialist statements:
(1) Pain is the firing of c-fibre
(2) heat is the motion of molecules
 
For Kripke, Identity statements between rigid designators are necessary if true. If they are *necessarily true* then there is no possible world in which they are false. "Pain" and "heat" are *rigid designators* = they pick out the same thing in every possible world.

But there is a felt contingency between them. It seems conceivable that (1) and (2) could be false, i.e. imagining heat without the motion of the molecules, or pain without the firing of c-fibres. 

Kripke argues that the felt contingency of (2) can be satisfactorily explained, but the same cannot be said about (1). 

When we imagine heat without molecular motion, we are really imagining a phenomena that affects our senses in the way that heat does but isn't molecular motion. There are two separate items:
- appearance: the feeling of warmth in your experience
- reality: molecular motion that causes it
Because they're separate, we can coherently pull them apart in imagination.

The same cannot be said for pain. The *sensation of pain* is *pain*. So imagining something that feels exactly like pain, without C-fibers, is imagining pain without C-fibers.

Therefore the felt contingency of (1) can’t be explained away, and psycho-physical identities look different from ordinary theoretical identities, which makes materialism implausible.

#### the explanatory gap

Levine points out that while statement (2) expresses an identity that is fully explanatory (with nothing crucial left out), the same cannot be said for statement (1). There is an explanatory gap.

The statement "pain is the firing of c-fibres" explains the causal role of pain. And while this is an important facet of what there is to be explained about pain, there is more to our concept of pain than its causal role. 

> "there is its qualitative character, how it feels; and what is left unexplained by the discovery of C-fiber firing is *why pain should feel the way it does*! For there seems to be nothing about C-fiber firing which makes it naturally “fit” the phenomenal properties of pain, any more than it would fit some other set of phenomenal properties." (Levine, 1983, p. 4)

> "the identification of the qualitative side of pain with C-fiber firing (or some property of C-fiber firing) leaves the connection between it and what we identify it with completely mysterious. One might say, it makes the way pain feels into merely a brute fact." (Levine, 1983, p. 4)

#### Consequences of the explanatory gap

> "Now, if there were some intrinsic connection discernible between having one’s C-fibers firing (or being in functional state F) and what it’s like to be in pain, by which I mean that experiencing the latter was intelligible in terms of the properties of the former, then we could derive our measure of similarity from the nature of the explanation. Whatever properties of the firing of C-fibers (or being in state F) that explained the feel of pain would determine the properties a kind of physical (or functional) state had to have in order to count as feeling like our pain. But without this explanatory gap filled in, facts about the kind or the existence of phenomenal experiences of pain in creatures physically (or functionally) different from us become impossible to determine. This, in turn, entails that the truth or falsity of (1), while perhaps metaphysically factual, is nevertheless epistemologically inaccessible. This seems to be a very undesirable consequence of materialism." (Levine, 1989, p. 7)

If there were an intrinsic, discernible connection between the physical or functional state and the feeling of pain, then the explanation would give us a measure of similarity. By this, he means that whatever features of c-fibre firing explains the painfulness of pain would define what any other kind of state must have in order to count as pain. For instance, the explanatory properties of molecular motion tells us what other systems count as hot. Without that explanation, nothing tells us which physical or functional difference matter. A creature with a different hardware might or might not be in pain, and we have no principled way to know. Hence, while statement (1) could be metaphysically true, it would nevertheless be “epistemologically inaccessible” in respect to other creatures. We could have complete physical / functional descriptions of an alien system, and yet still not know whether it feels anything, because we lack the bridge from those descriptions to phenomenal facts.

The materialist can no longer answer the argument simply by insisting on the metaphysical truth of identity, since the problem is not that the identity is false but that we cannot justify our confidence about bats, octopuses, or machines having phenomenal aspects of consciousness. This connects [[phenomenology|phenomenal-consciousness]] to the problem of other minds.

This argument rests on a standard we already apply everywhere in science. An identity statement (X is Y) is useful for two reasons: it is true, and it explains why X behaves the way it does. Levine argues that if we had the kind of explanation that connected c-fibre firing to the feel of pain, we would be able to tell which property of c-fibre firing is responsible for pain feeling like it does. We could then ask for any other creature whether it has that property, giving us a criterion for pain. But we don’t have that explanation, and hence cannot project the identity to new cases. Levine’s argument simply asks that the pain identity do what identities do everywhere else in science, making it harder to dismiss. Furthermore, by drawing out the gap’s consequences for creatures unlike us, Levine turns an abstract puzzle into a concrete problem and showcases that the pain identity is incomplete in a way that matters.

The explanatory gap hence is best understood not as a refutation of materialism, but as an unpaid debt, one that any account of consciousness, physical or otherwise, will eventually have to pay.
