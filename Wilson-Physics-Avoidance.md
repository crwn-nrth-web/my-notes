This essay argues that practical physics often achieves predictive and explanatory success through **“physics avoidance”**: transforming problems into more tractable explanatory frameworks rather than modeling every process directly.

The focus is “to investigate how various strategies for effective “physics avoidance” can alter the background explanatory landscape in which a problem is framed” (p. 2).

Examples:
- vibrations of a string = trading uncountably many particle positions $q(r)$ for a few harmonic modes $E_{i} , \theta_{i}$
- water flowing through a pipe = laminar steady states replace molecular tracking

Wilson highlights two main modes of physics avoidance:
1. appealing to time scaling considerations (e.g. equilibrium or steady state conditions) as a rationale for dropping terms from starting equation in a manner that alters their "hyperbolic signature"
2. adding non-hyperbolic equations to an equation set in the form of higher scale constraints

Wilson gives an illustrative example:
> "Jack and Jill have just fallen from the top of a steep hill; can one predict where they will land? “Sure--somewhere in the basin at the bottom.” In thinking thus, one is implicitly assuming that the kinetic energy they acquire in tumbling down the hill will be lost  in their encounters with rock and twig and so they will come to equilibrium (= bruised rest) in the potential well at the hill’s base. In reasoning so, we jump immediately ahead to the anticipated equilibrium state and forego any attempt to compute their paths of descent from hilltop to basin."
