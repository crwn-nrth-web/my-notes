### Discovering DNA as genetic material

Historical timeline of what constitutes a gene / what makes up genetic material =
- early scientists considered proteins to be the substance of genes
- In 1869, scientist identified 'nuclein' as a weakly acidic, phosphorous-rich material from the nuclei of human white blood cells.
- Its major component later turned out to be DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid).
- In 1923, the use of Schiff reagent, which stains DNA red, showed DNA to be almost exclusively located within the chromosomes.
- But the discovery that DNA was a chromosomal constituent did not establish that it had anything to do with genes. This had yet to be proven.

**Milestone experiment** - Frederick Griffith used bacteria to implicate DNA as genetic material (1923)
- easy to work with bacteria because bacteria carry their genetic material as a single circular chromosome without it being enclosed by a nuclear membrane
- Used *Streptococcus pneumoniae*
	- two forms = smooth (S) being virulent and wild-type, and rough (R) which is mutant
- Griffith injected *Streptococcus pneumoniae* into mice
	- S = lethal
	- R or heat-killed S = non-lethal
	- mixture of R and heat killed S = lethal (unexpected result). The dead mice contained live R and live S
- how could live R and dead S produce live S strains? 
	- he proposed a process of **transformation** = the ability of a substance to change the genetic characteristics of a cell
- other labs repeated the experiment and achieved transformation by simply growing R-form bacteria in the presence of components from dead S-forms (without mice)

- In 1944 Avery et al. set out to find component responsible for transformation 
	- prepared various purified and semi-purified components from the mixture and tested to see if they could transform R from bacteria
	- found the only one that could cause transformation was DNA
	- this suggested genes were made up of DNA

**Waring Blender Experiment**
- 1952, Hershey and Chase experimented with infecting bacterial cells with bacteriophages
- T2 bacteriophages are very small and are composed of equal weights of protein and DNA
- investigating: what substance directs the production of new phage particles, DNA or protein?

*Bacteriophage life cycle* =
1. phage attaches and injects genes into host bacterium
2. phage DNA replicated and new phage proteins are made
3. page particles assemble
4. cell bursts releasing new phage

Electron microscope revealed that when phage infect bacteria, they leave the viral shell (ghost) on the surface of the cell. The substance for phage replication was injected into the bacterial cells.

Hershey and Chase created two distinct batches of radioactively labelled phages =
1. Batch 1 (labelled protein) = phages + E.coli bacteria grown in medium containing sulfur-35
2. Batch 2 (labelled DNA) = phages + E.coli bacteria grown in medium containing phosphorus-32
These mediums were used because phosphorus is not present in proteins, and sulfur is not present in DNA

*Steps* =
- the labelled phages were allowed to attach to and infect E.coli bacteria
- A Waring blender was used to agitate the mixture, shearing the empty protein coats off the outside of the bacterial cells
- A centrifuge spun the mixture. The heavier infected bacteria cells formed a solid pellet at the bottom, while the lighter empty phage ghosts stayed on top

*Results* =
- For Batch 1, the radioactive sulfur stayed in the phage ghosts, meaning protein did not enter the bacterial cell
- For Batch 2, the radioactive phosphorus was found inside the bacterial cells in the pellet, showing that DNA had been injected into the host molecule
- hence, DNA is the hereditary molecule

## Molecular mechanism of DNA replication

Replication is a complex process which occurs at a precise moment in the cell cycle (S-phase in eukaryotes)

Synthesis is always 5' to 3' direction

Using E.coli of DNA replication as a model =
- E.coli has only one origin of replication (ori C), while eukaryotes have multiple origins

Two steps: 
1. **initiation**
	- the origin of replication is targeted by initiator (DNA-A) proteins
	- more DNA-A binds forming a complex with the origin 
	- DNA-B (DNA helicase) and DNA-C protein join the initiation complex and produce a replication bubble
	- DNA helicase catalyzes the unwinding of the parental double helix
	- single stranded binding (SBB) protein keeps the strands apart 
2. **elongation**
	- DNA polymerase needs a free 3' OH in order to add nucleotides, so the orientation of the strands is important
	- DNA primase (i.e. RNA polymerase) makes an RNA primer with a free 3' OH
	- DNA polymerase III extends DNA using RNA primers, making leading strands
	- additional RNA primers are added at the other ends as the replication bubble opens up, forming Okazaki fragments
	- The RNA primers are removed by DNA polymerase I , which simultaneously replaces them with DNA (5' to 3'), still leaving a gap between a 5' and 3' end
	- DNA ligase seals the 3' OH and 5' PO4 nicks by catalyzing the formation of polypeptides bonds. This completes the formation of the lagging strand

*leading strand* = synthesized continuously toward the replication fork
*lagging strand* = synthesized discontinuously in short segments called *Okazaki fragments* away from the replication fork



# Genetic code

**Gene expression**
- the flow of genetic information from DNA via RNA to protein
- information in DNA is transcribed into a messenger RNA where it gets translated into proteins (or polypeptides)

**translating from nucleotides to amino acids** = how do we go to 20 amino acids from 4 nucleotides?
- each nucleotide triplet is known as a *codon* that codes for one unique amino acid
- the start codon that begins the translation: *AUG = methionine* 
- stop codons: *UAA / UAG / UGA*
- the genetic code is degenerate = several codons will give you the same codons

- the Open Reading Frame is between the start and stop codon

### Frameshift mutations

**Frameshift mutations** = changes that alter the grouping of nucleotides into codons
- inserting or deleting nucleotides, not in multiples of three, shifts the reading frame and alters downstream amino acids
	- Adding a single nucleotide +1
	- Adding two nucleotides +2
	- etc.

**Intragenic suppression** = a nearby compensating insertion or deletion can restore the reading frame and yield a functioning protein of enough of the original sequence remains intact

![[Pasted image 20261121210052.png|578]] ![[Pasted image 20261121210943.png]]

If a gene sustains 3 (or multiple of 3) nucleotide mutations of the same sign (insertions or deletions), the protein can still function. 

This is only true only if the mutation does not affect the majority of amino acids
- if all 3 insertions/deletions happen close together at the beginning, only a tiny section is scrambled, leaving most of the reading frame functional
- having the insertions/deletions far apart keeps most of the protein altered, it becomes a non-functional mutant

## Transcription

**Transcription** = the process by which the polymerization of ribonucleotides guided by the complementary base pairings produces an RNA transcript of a gene
- going from DNA to mRNA
- nucleotides are added in the *5' to 3'* direction
- T is replaced with U in RNA

**RNA polymerase** = enzyme that catalyzes transcription

**Promoters** = DNA sequences near the the beginnings of genes that signal RNA polymerase where to begin the transcription

**Terminators** = sequences in the RNA products that tell RNA polymerase where to stop

In DNA, the start and stop codons are nested within the promoter and terminator regions. The mRNA transcribes the region between the promoter and terminator, including the start and stop codons. The region between the stop and start codons is the *open reading frame* (ORF)
