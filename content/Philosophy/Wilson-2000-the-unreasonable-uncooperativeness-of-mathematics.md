 Wilson begins by pointing out that it has become common to characterize mathematics as "the general science of possible structures". Within mathematics big bag of general "structures", there must be a structure that is structurally isomorphic to that of the physical world. 
 
> “I have defined *mathematical optimism* to be the doctrine that every real-life physical structure can be expected to possess a suitable direct representative within the world of mathematics.” (Wilson, 2000, p. 297)

Within mathematical optimism, he highlights two forms =
- *lazy optimism* = the assumption that "every real-life physical structure can [a priori] be expected to possess a suitable direct representative within the world of mathematics" (p. 297)
- *honest optimism* = every real-life physical structure can be expected to possess a suitable direct representative generated by concrete mathematical processes (Eulerian machinery, e.g. governing equations plus initial conditions spawning charts "on their own initiative")

Mapping the trajectory of a thrown ball:
- Lazy optimism = just setting a grid on the world (tuple-coding)
- honest optimism = using equations to generate the grid

He thinks that anti-realists have accepted "lazy optimism" and attack only its worst version, whereas the serious target is "honest optimism".

However, there has also been the opinion that only when the processes of nature have a special simplicity can mathematics track it adequately. Wilson calls this =

> "*mathematical opportunism*: it is the job of the applied mathematician to look out for the special circumstances that allow mathematics to say something useful about physical behavior." (Wilson, 2000, p. 297)

Mathematical opportunism is "based upon the belief that the success of applied mathematics require some alien element that cannot be regarded as invariably present in the physical world" 

The prime question of mathematical opportunism is to what extent do we succeed in representing reality with physics. 

Wilson's argument is that the difficulties in modelling nature that anti-realists treat as blows against science are actually facts about mathematics, not about the world, and so they should be reinterpreted as supporting mathematical opportunism rather than anti-realism. 

**Stance of the paper** =


wilson is against analytic philosophy. If you try to simplify things, even by just providing a general framework of how science works, then we lose the specific details that is involved in applied mathematics
###### d'Alembert example

> “the applicability of the classical equation for a vibrating string, which had been developed by d'Alembert. This formula contains a second derivative reflecting the string's change of shape. Intuitively, one expects that a string can be plucked into a "V" configuration. But the equation's reliance upon the second derivative makes it seem as if it can't possibly make sense for a string containing a sharp bend. D'Alembert concluded, in an admirably opportunist spirit, that, although the bent string represents a genuine physical possibility, mathematics cannot expect to treat such a case and must restrict its attention to strings that are regular enough to possess second derivatives. . .
> . . . His reasoning is better parsed as follows: "Claims about infinitesimal relationships as embodied in the wave equation make sense only if they represent limiting distillates of some global regularity displayed in the macroscopic behavior at hand. An infinitesimal claim about tangents, for example, must derive its meaning from the pattern displayed in some regular geometrical figure such as an ellipse or hyperbola. But, there is no reason why nature should need, except by”  (Wilson, 2000, p. 298)

Calculus only works on things that are already smooth everywhere. A tangent at a point on an ellipse or circle makes sense because the entire curve is smooth and regular. The local fact depends on the global pattern. However, nature doesn't have to be smooth, as seen by the fact that the sharp-bent string is perfectly possible in the real world. 

Wilson argues that d'Alembert's point is: math tools comes with built in requirements (smoothness, regularity, etc.) that the real world doesn't always meet. Wilson calls these *"alien elements"* = something that the maths brings along that nature isn't obligated to provide.

This example showcases the two sides:
- *opportunism* (d'Alembert's view) = since math only works where the world happens to be regular, mathematicians should look for those special cases and stick to them. Math can't handle everything
- *optimism* (Euler's view) = math can handle everything in principle. Euler realized that a differential equation can build the answer step by step on its own, without needing a pre-existing smooth formula

###### Core vs. Satellite equations

*core equations* = equations built directly from fundamental physical principles. Taking the set of core equations, if supplied both initial conditions and adequate boundary conditions, will dictate how the object will evolve over time (evolutionary type)

Core equations are the thing that changes through scientific revolutions. 

*Satellite equations* = because core equations can be hard to solve, scientists typically adopt an "equilibrium strategy", resulting in equations losing the hyperbolic character of the core equations. 

> “In other words, when we employ the "satellite" equilibrium equations instead of the original core modeling, we are in effect saying to the physical world: "Look, I don't care how you get across this muddy field; I'll meet you on the other side of the stile." In contrast, the "core equations" steadfastly attempt to plot each mucky step and slip all the way to the point of rendezvous.” (Wilson, 2000, p. 307)
