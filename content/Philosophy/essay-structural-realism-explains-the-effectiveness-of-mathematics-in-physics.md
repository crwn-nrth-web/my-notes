An important question for scientists is whether our theories aim to provide an accurate description of the world, especially when it comes to unobservable entities. Two main arguments have shaped this debate. The no miracles argument holds that the empirical success of our best theories must mean that they have latched onto the blueprint of the universe, rather than it being a coincidence or a miracle. The pessimistic meta-induction argument asks what rational basis we have for believing that our current theories describe the world, given that past successful theories were abandoned and are now regarded as false. 

Worrall offers [[structural-realism]] as “the best of both worlds”: a defensible form of realism that still gives pessimistic meta-induction argument its full weight. I argue that structural realism is compelling not only for this reason, but because of an advantage Worrall left unstated: it offers a plausible explanation of why mathematics is so successful in physics.

Worrall notes that realism is plausible only if science has been "essentially cumulative", i.e. deposed theories themselves (not just their successful empirical consequences) have lived on in some way. He argues that they have, but at the level of structure. What survives theory change are the relational structures, rather than the claims about what the underlying entities are. 

The standard example is the replacement of Fresnel’s elastic-solid ether theory of light by Maxwell’s electromagnetism. The picture of light as vibrations in a mechanical ether was abandoned, but Fresnel’s equations for the relative intensities of reflected and refracted light carried over intact. Equations are our access point, and are the visible testable evidence of a mathematical structure being preserved. What Fresnel's theory got right was that structure. The quantities in the equations were reinterpreted as electric and magnetic field strengths, while the mathematical relations among them stayed the same. 

Worrall concedes that this case is not the best representation, since the equations are preserved exactly. More commonly, old equations reappear only as limiting cases of the new theory (e.g. relativistic mechanics tend towards Newtonian mechanics when v/c approaches to 0). This still supports structural realism, because the realist claims approximate truth, not exact truth. Old equations arising as limiting cases showcase how they were approximately correct, with the new theory explaining why and where they fail. In other words, the best explanation of the old equations' success is that they approximately captured real structures. 

This continuity is what the no-miracles argument needs: the old theories were successful because they latched onto real relations, even while misdescribing the relata. 

This is an epistemic view: the world may contain objects with intrinsic natures, but all we can know are their structural relations. Conversely, ontic structural realism holds that structure is all there is. The epistemic reading is more modest and, I think, better supported: it takes the physics at face value without converting a limit on what theories tell us into a claim about what exists.

The trajectory of modern physics also provides support towards structural realism. In quantum mechanics, identical particles are indistinguishable in a way that classical objects are not, so the theory attends to symmetry of the state rather than the identity of individual particles. What we can say about electrons is how it transforms under certain symmetry groups, what is conserved, and how it interacts. Each step towards a more fundamental theory has traded picture of entities with intrinsic properties for a more relational, more mathematical description. 

If we take mathematics to be the study of structures ([[mathematical-structuralism]]), where objects are just placeholders with no nature beyond their place in the pattern, and our scientific theories aim to capture relations, then it is no coincidence that they fit so well. Mathematics is not an arbitrary tool that happens to work, but the appropriate tool for stating what theories get right. Consider the inverse-square law: Newtonian gravitation and Coulomb’s electrostatics share one relation, force falling with the square of distance, though the relata differ (masses, charges). Mathematics can state that relation once because it leaves the relata open.

This also explains why mathematics can be developed before its applications are known. Imagine an atlas containing maps for every possible territory, drawn without knowing which territories exist. When a real territory is finally discovered, it is not miraculous that one of the maps already fits. Mathematics could be described as this atlas of possible structures, developed largely independently of any particular application, and physics is the discovery of which of these structures best describes the world’s relations, at least approximately.

[[Wigner-and-Islami-on-the-unreasonable-effectiveness-of-mathematics-in-physics|Islami-interpretation]] of modern physics further develops why the structure physics capture is mathematical. On her view, the distinguishing feature of modern physics is the central role of invariance principles. The fact there are regularities in events motivate the formulation for laws of nature, and regularities in those laws lead to invariance principles which mathematics is particularly well suited to representing. In this sense, mathematics is effective in physics because the structure of modern physical laws is one that mathematical structures are capable of expressing. If there were no regularities in nature, mathematics would not have been as successful in the formulation of our scientific theories. This fits Worrall’s structural realism. If theories keep what is relational and invariant, then it is unsurprising that the part of the theory that endures is the part mathematics describes best. Structural realism hence offers a plausible explanation of mathematics success: the equations approximately capture real relations in the world.
