In the 1950s and 1960s, several philosophers noted that scientists needed additional guidance for theory choice than just logic and evidence alone. So, the term **value-free science** really refers to the norm of epistemic values only in the internal stages of science. 

**external parts of science** = research ethics before project begins and tech applications afterwards

**internal parts of science** = during the course of research itself (e.g. within the lab and writing up the results)

**epistemic values** = term to encompass the values acceptable in science as guidance for theory choice because they contribute to scientific knowledge
- e.g. = accuracy of instruments, measures of certainty and uncertainty, parsimony (simpler theories are better than complicated theories), logical consistency, falsifiability for [[popper-1934-logic-of-scientific-discovery]], being maximally transparent about research results
- these values can be debated of what counts as epistemic values (like parsimony or falsifiability)

**non-epistemic values** = required in the "external" parts of science (choice of projects, limitations of methodology like use of human subjects, and the application of science related technologies)
- e.g. = political beliefs, what research should be conducted on human/non-human subjects, choosing to do research based on availability of funding (profit-motive), activities that are considered valuable for reasons other knowledge (e.g. prestige, addressing problems for human beings), ethical values

**Thesis** = for policy relevant science, we need to reject the value-free ideal of science as value-free science in this sense is "unacceptable". Non-epistemic values are logically needed for reasoning in science and that the rejection of the value-free ideal does not demolish science's objectivity

**Appeal of value-free ideal** = 
- want to make sure scientists are not making ethical decisions (because others are more qualified)
- protects scientists from thinking about ethical questions (shield from blame)
- Douglas notes that the ideal persists because if we accept the presence of values (particularly non-epistemic values) in science, it would be a fatal blow to objectivity.  [[logical-empiricism]] wants scientific knowledge to be based on evidence alone with no effect from subjective values (p. 5)
# Section 2: Choices and values in science
[[Kuhn-1962-structure-of-scientific-revolutions]] points out that scientists make choices and interpretations. The paradigm in fact shapes the interpretation and choices made by scientists.

Douglas also points this out that in doing science, scientists must make choices (e.g choosing a particular method, decisions on how to characterize events for recording date, decision on how to interpret the results). In fact, scientific papers are structured along these lines (methodology, data, result) but scientists rarely explicitly discuss the choices that they make. They rarely mention the alternative paths that they might have taken. 

> "To discuss the choices that they make would  require some justification for those choices, and this is territory the scientist would prefer  to avoid . . . Because scientists do not recognize a  legitimate role for values in science (it would damage “objectivity”), scientists avoid  discussion of the choices that they make." p. 6

**how do choices require the consideration of epistemic and non-epistemic values** = any choice involves the possibility for error and if one is to weigh which errors are more serious, one will need to assign values to the various likely consequences. Thus, values become an important factor in the making of internal scientific choices.

In general, if there is widely recognized uncertainty and thus a significant chance of error, we hold people responsible to consider the consequences of error when making their decision-making process. 
- e.g. the emergency room avoids as much as possible any false negatives with respect to potential heart attack victims; the justice system attempts to avoid false positive

Douglas notes that we might decide to isolate scientists from having to think about the consequences of their errors, but in this argument, she supposes that we want to hold scientists to the same standards as everyone else, and thus, scientists should think about the potential consequences of error.

It is important to note that Douglas makes it clear that evidence and values are not the same thing. 
- Values are statements of norms, goals and desires
- Evidence consists of descriptive statements about the world
- value judgements are needed to determine whether that descriptive label is accurate enough
##### Rat liver tissue example
Rats were exposed to a chemical dioxin and after two years of dosing, the rats were killed and autopsied. Slides were re-evaluated by three different groups. The 3 different analyses of rates of rat cancer produced different results because they applied different standards of error (false positives vs false negatives).

What does this uncertainty mean for the decision? 
- if we consider too many false negatives, then dioxin may appear as a less potent carcinogen, leading to weaker regulations
- too many false positives will make dioxin appear to be more potent and dangerous, leading to burdensome and unnecessary overregulation

The concept that this is demonstrating is that when scientists draw conclusions, they risk **false positives** and **false negatives**. Deciding how much evidence is "enough" depends on how bad would it be to falsely accept this claim? how bad would it be to falsely reject it? and these are non-epistemic value judgements.
### why do values need to be considered as a part of science
Douglas argues that the ideal of value-free science in unattainable because scientists should be held responsible and must make decisions under uncertainty that involve weighing the consequences of error. These decisions require value judgements. Pretending science is value-free does not eliminate values from science, it merely hides them, making them less transparent and less democratically unaccountable. Values, nevertheless creep back in "subconsciously". Therefore, rather than excluding values, science should acknowledge and critically manage their role. 

> "if we want to hold scientists to the same responsibilities the rest of us have, the  judgments needed to do science cannot escape the consideration of potential  consequences, both intended and unintended, both epistemically relevant and socially  relevant." p. 10

# Section 3: Scientists, Responsibility and Autonomy
Douglas notes that there are two general objections to her position:
1. scientists shouldn't make choices involving value judgements; they should do science concerned with epistemic values only and leave determining the implications of their work to policy makers
2. we should shield scientists from having to think about the consequences of error in their work in order to protect the value neutrality of the scientific process

Douglas also notes that scientists performing the research may be the only ones who are both aware of the uncertainties and potential for error and the likely consequences  of that error. E.g. several theoretical physicists realized that there was a possibility a nuclear explosion might ignite the atmosphere. This potential of error could only have been considered by those experts, not outsiders.
#### objection 1 - policy makers should make the non-epistemic value judgements

An argument for scientists not needed to consider values is that 
1. they are not the ones performing the decisions for which the consequences of error are relevant
2. they are simply reporting their data for the use of decision-makers

Douglas' [[#Rat liver tissue example]] shows that even in the act of reporting "raw" data, some choices need to be made by the scientist on how to characterize events. These choices involve potential for error and so even "raw" data can include judgements that require values in the process.

Also, it is rare for scientists to solely report raw data to policy makers because policy-makers rarely have the requisite expertise to interpret data and it is more fitting that scientists are called upon to make sense of their own data. Interpretation, however, requires value considerations. For instance, selecting background assumptions require epistemic and non-epistemic considerations, like which kinds of errors are more likely given different assumptions and how to weigh the seriousness of the errors.

> "In short, we  cannot effectively use scientific information without scientific interpretation, but  interpretation involves value considerations. And few outside the scientific community  are equipped to make those interpretations. So scientists usually must interpret their  findings for policy-makers and the public." p. 13

#### objection 2 - scientists should be shielded from value judgements
Some argue that scientists should be set apart from the general moral requirements that most of us are held to and that scientists should be required solely to search for truth. 

Douglas interprets this to mean that "scientists may make dubious choices with severe consequences  of error, but we would not ask them to think about those consequences and would not  hold them responsible if and when they occur."

Douglas highlights two possibilities for this to be possible:
1. if we thought that epistemic values always trumped social values
2. if someone else (with the authority to  make decisions regarding research choices) could step up to consider non-epistemic values and social consequences
For Douglas, if both of these options fail, the burden of responsibility to consider all relevant potential consequences of one's choices falls back to the scientist

For the first option (epistemic values trump other kinds of values), it is clear that this is not the case. For instance, there are limits on the use of human/non-human subjects in research, test bans on nuclear weapons, etc., which shows that we are not willing to sacrifice all for the search for truth. Debate on the appropriate budget for federally-funded research also shows that we do weigh epistemic values against other considerations. In other words, **epistemic values is not the end-all-be-all**.

The second option is possible but it has its in difficulties, some discussed in [[#objection 1 - policy makers should make the non-epistemic value judgements]]. The cost of non-epistemic research overseen by outsiders usually outweighs the potential benefits. Douglas notes that for this option to be possible, the third party would need to be involved in every stage of the scientific process, basically looking over the scientists shoulder, which strips the scientists of their decision-making autonomy. 

> "This division of authority would  likely lead to resentment among the scientists and to a reduced reflection by scientists on  the potential consequences of research. After all, increased reflection would only  complicate the scientist’s research by requiring more intensive consultation with the  ethical overseer." p. 16

# Section 4: Implications for objectivity-and-science
Douglas argues that science is still objective in the senses that matter 
- intersubjectivity
- investigation of external world
