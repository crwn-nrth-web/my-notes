The **inferential conception of applied mathematics** is a extension of [[Pincock-2004-New-perspective-on-the-problem-of-applying-mathematics|Pincock-mapping-account]], which claims that “the existence of an appropriate mapping from a mathematical structure to a physical structure is sufficient to fully explain the particular application of the mathematical structure in question.” (p. 346)

The paper argues that the mapping account is correct to say that “Mathematics is a rich source of structures and when a mathematical theory finds  applications in empirical science, it is clear that the mathematics captures certain important structural relations of the system in question” (p. 348)  but it is incomplete in some ways. 

**isomorphism** = a mapping account is that two things are structurally identical to one another
- this is what [[Pincock-2004-New-perspective-on-the-problem-of-applying-mathematics|Pincock-mapping-account]] is based on 
###### Analogy of mathematics as a street map

**Why is mathematics so useful in empirical science?** = "one answer is that mathematics is a rich source of structure and therein lies its utility"

> "On this view, the usefulness of mathematics is much like the usefulness of a city street map. Once the structural similarity between the map and the city's streets is established, the map can stand in for the world. Moreover, various hard-to-determine facts about the city can be read off rather easily from the map. All that is required for a map to be useful is that it faithfully  represents some aspects of the city in question. It is important to note that the map need not faithfully preserve all structural relations. . . . Indeed, much of the utility of the map derives from the fact that  itis quite deliberately less complex than the city it represents. In other words, the city has more structure than is represented in the map. It is also worth mentioning that for a street map to be accurate, there must be a structure-preserving mapping (in the mathematical sense) between the city and its map (in the usual, non-technical sense)."

#### Problems with the mapping account

*What we mean by "structure of the world"* 
- defining structure to be "a set of objects (or nodes or positions) and a set of relations on these" (p. 347)[^1]
- the physical world doesn't come equipped with a sets of objects and set of relations on those. There are either constructs of our theories of the world or identified by our theories of the world
- if we can divvy up the world into such things, this would be identified by our theories

> “What we require for the mapping account to get started is something like a pre-theoretic structure of the world (or at least a pre-modeling structure of the world) . . . the mapping account does require having what we shall call an assumed structure in order to get started.” (p. 347)

*what if the solutions to the mathematical problem have no empirical content?*
- “the purpose of using the mathematics is to model the empirical situation and then presumably solve problems in the mathematics that correspond to empirical problems.”
- the problem is which parts of mathematical structure represent and which parts do not?
- e.g. for determining where a projectile will land, the displacement function is quadratic with two real solutions, but only one is physically significant
- it is almost always clear which solution is physically significant (provided by physical intuitions such as the projectile cannot land in two places and it will typically land forward of the launch site) but this information that is required to solve the physical problem is not part of the mapping between the mathematical structure and the physical structure

> “very often the mathematics employed either captures more physical structure than was intended, or solutions that, from physical considerations alone, appear not to be physically significant yet turn out to  be physically significant. As Heinrich Hertz once suggested, mathematics, it seems, is wiser than we are.  This is one aspect of what has become known as "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics"[^2]” (p. 350)

*known mismatch between the empirical structure and the mathematical structure*
- the mapping account doesn't explain idealizations (such as those made in fluid dynamics)

*mathematics being explanatory*
- the mapping account implies that mathematics is solely a representational system
- mathematics can play a role in the unification of otherwise disparate scientific theories, in making novel predictions, and in providing mathematical explanations
- if mathematics is genuinely explanatory, then this will be a problem for the mapping account

> “Consider once again our map of a city. Certain facts about the city will be more obvious in the street map- indeed, that's the purpose of a street map- but it would be very odd to think of the map as providing an  explanation of any facts about the city.” (p. 351)

#### Types of mapping

**isomorphism** = everything is mapped onto something else. The two objects are structurally identical

**homomorphism** = things map onto something else, but there are certain features in one domain that might not have any relation to anything in the other domain

**partial mappings** = relation R consists of $<R_{1}, R_{2}, R_{3}>$ where 
- $R_{1}$ = relations that (we know) holds
- $R_{2}$ = relations that (we know) do not hold
- $R_{3}$ = relations that are undetermined (either ontologically or epistemically)
It is the $R_3$ relation that makes it a partial mapping

> “However, even in idealized contexts, certain aspects of the actual empirical situation can still be mapped to relevant features of the mathematical structures invoked. The R1 and R2 components of the partial relations in the empirical set up are mapped via the relevant partial isomorphism or partial homomorphism into the corresponding partial relations in the mathematical structure. However, the R3 components are left open. These components correspond to the features of the idealization that bring mismatches between the actual empirical world and the mathematical model.” (Bueno and Colyvan, 2011, p. 359)

### An inferential conception of applied mathematics

**Key features of the inferential conception** =
- the fundamental role of applied mathematics is inferential i.e. “the ability to establish inferential relations between empirical phenomena and mathematical structures, or among mathematical structures themselves.” (p. 351)
- This account accommodates idealizations (via partial mapping)
- it accommodates the multiple roles that mathematical theories play in applied contexts (the role of mathematical explanations)
- illustrates how the account explains which parts of the mathematical models refer and which do not
- accommodates structures other than causal structures (asymptotic reasoning)

This gives us a way to use *inferences* in our explanation of how we understand the world. Rather than saying we have found truth from mathematics, there is more emphasis on human-centric inferences from mathematical structures to physical structures. I feel like this is reducing the constraint by going from discovering truth to making inferences. Another way it is more modest is by introducing partial mapping rather than full mapping. 

**Three-step scheme** (p. 353)
1. *immersion* = "establishing a mapping from the empirical set up to a convenient mathematical structure"
2. *derivation* = "drawing consequences from the mathematical formalism, using the mathematical structure obtained in the first step"
3. *interpretation* = "interpreting the mathematical consequences in terms of the initial empirical set up"

e.g. we take an empirical set up (projectile motion) and map it to a suitable mathematical formalism (equations of velocity, acceleration, distance) = *immersion*. We then draw consequences by solving the equation to find distance = *derivation*. We then interpret these solutions to determine the distance the projectile travels = *interpretation*.

- the empirical set up is the relevant bits of the empirical world, not a mathematical-free description of it

###### example from economics
- in neo-classical economics, agents are modelled as being perfectly rational. Though, this is not how agents behave in the world
- there is a full mapping between the behavior of idealized agents and certain mathematical structures of analysis
- the relevant mathematical equations can then be solved
- th results are then mapped back into the behavior of idealized agents
- However, it is important to recognize that there is partial mapping between the behavior of actual agents and the relevant mathematical structures

##### How the inferential account explains mathematics multiple roles

> "One way of thinking about the use of mathematics in unification, novel  predictions, and mathematical explanations is in terms of epistemic accessibility, highlighting the function that inferences play in all of these issues.  In fact, unification, novel predictions, and explanations are ultimately due to the inferential role of mathematics."
