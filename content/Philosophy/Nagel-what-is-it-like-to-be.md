> Nagel, T. (1974). What Is It Like to Be a Bat? _The Philosophical Review_, _83_(4), p. 435 - 450. [https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914)

Nagel argues that while Physicalism might not be false, science is currently unable to tell us anything about our [[phenomenology|subjectivity-and-phenomenology]].

> "At the present time the status of physicalism is similar to that which the hypothesis that matter is energy would have had if uttered by a pre-Socratic philosopher. We do not have the beginnings of a conception of how it might be true" (p. 447)
#### What is it like to be a bat?
We have reason to think that bats have experiences, but it is hard to imagine what their experiences would be like.

> “an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism -- something it is like *for* the organism. We may call this the subjective character of experience.” (p. 436)

We can know a lot about bats from an objective standpoint (e.g. navigating by echo-location, cognitive capacities that allow bats to echo-locate). But we still won't know "what it is like to be a bat" -- how the world is phenomenally experienced by the bat from its subjective point of view. Somethings can only be known from the subjective perspective.

It will not help to try to imagine "that one had webbings on one's arm, . . . , that one has very poor vision, and perceives the surrounding world by a system of reflected high-frequency sound signals" etc. because it tells me only what it would be like for *me* to behave like a bat, not what it is like for a *bat* to be a bat.

> "We cannot form more than a schematic conception of what it *is* like. For example, we may ascribe general types of experience on the basis of the animal's structure and behavior. Thus we describe bat sonar as a form of three-dimensional forward perception; we believe that bats feel some versions of pain, fear, hunger, and lust, and that they have other, more familiar types of perception besides sonar. But we believe that these experiences also have in each case a specific subjective character, which it is beyond our ability to conceive." (p. 439)

Nagel distinguishes between two kinds of **inaccessible facts**:
1. *contingently inaccessible facts* = facts humans will never happen to grasp, because of limited time or circumstances. The concepts were available in principle to us; we just might not have gotten to them.
2. *constitutively inaccessible facts* = facts we couldn’t grasp even if humans lasted forever, because our cognitive architecture doesn’t support the relevant type of concept.

Trying to imagine what it is like to be a bat (or anything that is not ourselves) is a *constitutively inaccessible fact*; we cannot form more than a schematic conception of what it is like and so human concepts may be structurally incapable of capturing them.

Nagel argues that experiential facts (what it is like) are essentially tied to a subjective point of a view. Physicalism however aims at objectivity, which means describing things in a way that doesn't depend on any particular observer's viewpoint. Therefore, it is "a mystery" how a perspective-bound fact could be captured by a perspective-free account. Normally, in reductionism we set aside how the object appears to us, leaving behind the objective nature which the physical theory explain. With experience, this move fails because the appearance is the phenomenon and so there is no objective ore left behind.

> "After all, what would be left of what it was like to be a bat if one removed the viewpoint of the bat?" (p. 443)
#### Nagel's proposal for science of consciousness

> "At present we are completely unequipped to think about the subjective character of experience without relying on the imagination -- without taking up the point of view of the experiential subject. This should be regarded as a challenge to form new concepts and devise a new method -- an objective phenomenology not dependent on empathy or the imagination."
