An important question for scientists to ask themselves is whether or not our scientific theories aim to provide an accurate description of the world. Two main arguments have shaped this debate. The no miracles argument holds that the empirical success of our present theories must mean that they have latched onto the blueprint of the universe, rather than it being lucky guesses or a miracle. On the other hand, the pessimistic meta induction argument asks what rational basis we have for believing that current theories describe the world, given that past successful theories were abandoned and are now regarded as false. 

Worrall offers structural realism as the "the best of both worlds", a defensible form of realism while still giving the argument from scientific revolutions its full weight. I will argue that it also offers an insight Worrall left unstated, namely why mathematics works so well in physics.

**Worrall's argument**

Pessimistic meta induction compels the realist to ask whether the history of science supports the view that successive scientific theories are increasingly good approximations to the truth. Worrall notes that realism is plausible only if science has been "essentially cumulative", i.e. deposed theories themselves (not just their successful empirical consequences) have lived on in some way. He argues that they have, but at the level of structure. What survives theory change are the relational structures, rather than the claims about what the underlying entities are. 

The standard example is the replacement of Fresnel’s elastic-solid ether theory of light by Maxwell’s electromagnetism. The picture of light as vibrations in a mechanical ether was abandoned, but Fresnel’s equations for the relative intensities of reflected and refracted light carried over intact. Equations are our access point, and are the visible testable evidence of a mathematical structure being preserved. What Fresnel's theory got right was that structure. The quantities in the equations were reinterpreted as electric and magnetic field strengths, while the mathematical relations among them stayed the same. 

Worrall concedes that this case is not the best representation, since the equations are preserved exactly. More commonly, old equations reappear only as limiting cases of the new theory (e.g. relativistic mechanics tend towards Newtonian mechanics when v/c approaches to 0). This still supports structural realism, because the realist claims approximate truth, not exact truth. Old equations arising as limiting cases showcase how they were approximately correct, with the new theory explaining why and where they fail. In other words, the best explanation of the old equations' success is that they approximately captured real structures. 

This continuity is what the no-miracles argument needs: the old theories were successful because they latched onto real relations, even while misdescribing the relata. I find this a compelling compromise. It concedes what the history of science forces us to concede, while keeping realism rather than retreating to instrumentalism.

It is important to distinguish ontic from epistemic structural realism. Worrall's version is epistemic: the world may contain objects with intrinsic natures, but all we can know are their structural relations. Conversely, ontic structural realism holds that structure is all there is. The epistemic reading is more modest and, I think, better supported: it takes the physics at face value without converting a limit on what theories tell us into a claim about what exists.

---

Much of modern theoretical physics also seems to be moving in direction that structural realism describes. In quantum mechanics, identical particles are indistinguishable in a way that classical objects are not, so the theory attends to symmetry of the state rather than the identity of individual particles. What we can say about electrons is how it transforms under certain symmetry groups, what is conserved, and how it interacts. In general relativity, general covariance shows that spacetime points have no existing individuality and are as such relational. Each step towards a more fundamental theory has traded picture of entities with intrinsic properties for a more relational, more mathematical description. 

---
Worrall left this unstated, but I believe that structural realism has an additional benefit of explaining why mathematics is so effective in fitting into our physical theories. If we are to consider that our best scientific theories aim to capture relations, and  mathematics to be the study of structures with the relata open, then it is no coincidence that they fit.

The world might have been such that no mathematical structure fit in. This is best explained by Islami's interpretation that the distinguishing feature of modern physics is the central role of invariance principles. The fact there are regularities in events motivate the formulation for laws of nature, and regularities in those laws lead to invariance principles which mathematics is particularly well suited to representing. In this sense, mathematics is effective in physics not simply because mathematical concepts happen to correspond to physical reality, but because the structure of modern physical laws is one that mathematical structures are capable of expressing. If there were no regularities in nature, mathematics would not have been as successful in the formulation of our scientific theories. Combined with Worrall's point that what survives theory change is structure, the best explanation of that pattern is that the mathematical structure expressed by the equations approximately captures real relations in the world.


Taking physics' aim is to capture structure, rather than the nature of entities and mathematics as the study of structures with the relata open, the effectiveness of mathematics in physics becomes less of a mystery. What physics needs and what mathematics supplies are the same kind of thing. 

I see this as two separate things: there are the relational structure in the world and the mathematical structure that represents it in the form of equations.

Wigner described the effectiveness of mathematics in physics as unreasonable. But the puzzle presupposes that mathematics and physical content are independent domains which happen to match. Structural realism removes that presupposition. It claims that science describes structures of the world (not objects). Equations and models are what captures the relations (structures) between objects.
