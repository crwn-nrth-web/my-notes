The general idea behind naturalism is that all of our legitimate knowledge is acquired in a scientific way. Mathematical naturalism treats mathematics as a part of the natural world and of human practice, not as a realm of abstract objects known by special intuition.

## Kitcher

Traditional view of mathematics is grounded in an *apriorist epistemology* = certain knowledge can be attained independently of empirical (sensory experience),  relying on reason and logic.

A question for aprior traditions is how does a person know the axioms of mathematics?

**Naturalist account of mathematical knowledge** =
> “Our present body of mathematical beliefs is justified in virtue of its relation to a prior body of beliefs; that prior body of beliefs is justified in virtue of its relation to a yet earlier corpus; and so it goes. Somewhere, of course, the chain must be grounded. . . [there is] a state of rudimentary mathematical knowledge in which people are justified through their perceptual experiences in situations where they manipulate their environments. . . . What naturalism has to show is that contemporary mathematical knowledge results from this primitive state through a sequence of rational transitions” (Kitcher, 1988, p. 299)

Mathematical knowledge is passed down through a community, and each stage is justified by the previous one. 

**Five components of mathematical practice** (p. 299) =
1. language employed by the mathematicians whose practice it is
2. a set of statements accepted by those mathematicians
3. a set of questions that they regard as important and as currently unsolved
4. a set of reasonings that they use to justify the statements they accept
5. set of mathematical view embodying their ideas about how mathematics should be done, the ordering of mathematical disciplines, etc.

Kitcher's view is showing mathematics as a practice, rather than a truth or abstract notion. 

Kitcher regards the history of mathematics as a sequence of *rational* changes in mathematical practices. Contemporary mathematical practice can be connected with the primitive, empirically grounded practice through a chain of rational transitions. He outlines two types of transitions:
1. *internal transitions* = changes in components based on the practice alone
2. *external transitions* = changes in components due to outside influences

Science is assumed to be of external transitions, and mathematics to be of internal transitions. But this is not entirely true. Theoretical sciences are often changed through attempts to resolve tensions in the prevailing practice (Copernicus' efforts to resolve the problems of the planet), and the development of mathematics is affected by the state of other sciences, or ordinary experiences (pursuit of analysis was modified through the study of problems in theoretical physics).

> “By emphasizing the connection between transitions in the history of mathematics and transitions in the history of science that we view as rational, it is possible to argue for a reduction of the epistemology of mathematics to the epistemology of science.” (Kitcher, 1988, p. 301)

Kitcher makes arguments to remove the need for **rational intuitions** = direct, mental grasp of a self-evident, logical, or mathematical truth as a source of  a priori knowledge. 

> “if we treat mathematics as an idealized science of human operations.33 The ultimate subject matter of mathematics is the way in which human beings structure the world, either through performing crude physical manipulations or through operations of thought.” (Kitcher, 1988, p. 313)

> “Mathematical progress, in a nutshell, consists in constructing a systematic and idealized account of the operations that humans find it profitable to perform in organizing their experience.” (Kitcher, 1988, p. 315)

## Maddy

> “Paradoxical as it may sound, it now appears that even applied mathematics is pure” (Maddy, 2008, p. 33)

Maddy begins by giving an historical account:
- Plato saw mathematical knowledge as the study of eternal and unchaning abstract forms, while science is uncertain and changeable opinion about the world
- Galileo, and later the scientific revolution, takes mathematics and science to be the same. Mathematics become the substance of scientific theories; it is the language through which our scientific theories are described i.e. the great Book of Nature is written in mathematical language
- contemporary philosophy of science takes mathematical models to be highly idealized representation of the world (which is complicated). We have constructed mathematical models to capture aspects of empirical phenomena

**Historical rise of pure mathematics** = Maddy focuses on how mathematics came to peel away from the natural sciences and the shift from the attitude of mathematics describing the universe towards an attitude of pure mathematics, where Mathematicians began introducing concepts that had little or no direct physical meaning.

> “At that point, it became natural to regard mathematicians as providing a well-stocked warehouse of abstract structures from which the natural scientist is free to select whichever tool best suits his needs in representing the world.” (Maddy, 2008, p. 20)

It is hard to judge Truth when it comes to mathematics, as long as the axioms within it are not contradictory. Consider Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries, just because Euclidean geometry does not conform to the physical world, it is not wrong in a mathematical sense. We still pursue Euclidean geometry. The nature of mathematics does not seem to progress for a search of truth. Maddy argues that seems to be less external criteria that guides what is studied in mathematics.
