**Illusionism** = “the view that [[phenomenology|phenomenal-consciousness]], as usually conceived, is illusory. According to illusionists, our sense that it is like something to undergo conscious experiences is due to the fact that we systematically misrepresent them (or, on some versions, their objects) as having phenomenal properties.” (Frankish, p. 11)

**illusion problem** = the problem of explaining why experiences seem to have phenomenal properties 

When we encounter something that seems anomalous (radically inexplicable within our established scientific world-view), we have three options =
1. accept the phenomena is real and explore the implications of its existence, proposing major revisions or extensions to our science, perhaps leading to a paradigm shift
2. ague that although the phenomena is real, it is not in fact anomalous and can be explained within current science
3. argue that the phenomena is illusory and set about investigating how the illusion is produced

The first two options are realist ones, while the third is an illusionist one. 

The above options reflect attitudes towards phenomenal consciousness =
1. *radical realism* = treats phenomenal consciousness as real and inexplicable without radical theoretical innovation
	- e.g. dualists, neutral monists, Mysterians, etc.
2. *conservative realism* = accepts the reality of phenomenal consciousness but seeks to explain it in physical terms, using the resources of contemporary cognitive science or modest extensions of it
3. *Illusionism* = treats phenomenal consciousness as illusory, and denies that experiences have phenomenal properties 

It is important to note that Frankish defines phenomenal consciousness to have phenomenal properties that are characterized as "simple, ineffable, intrinsic, private, and immediately apprehended, and many theorists argue that they are distinct from all physical properties, inaccessible to third person science, and inexplicable in physical terms." (p. 13)

**quasi-phenomenal properties** = a non-phenomenal physical property that introspection typically misrepresents as phenomenal

**Strong illusionism** = it is an illusion to think that there are phenomenal properties at all. Introspectable properties of experiences are merely quasi-phenomenal ones

**weak illusionism** = holds that there are phenomenal properties, though it is an illusion to think that they are ineffable, intrinsic, etc.

For the illusionist, introspection delivers a partial, distorted view of our experiences, misrepresenting complex physical features as phenomenal ones. 

Illusionism is not denying that there is conscious experience. Simply, that there is no phenomenal properties, only quasi-phenomenal properties. Illusionism is also not necessarily arguing to eliminate talk of phenomenal properties or consciousness. Talk of the feel or quality of an experience could surely remain in everyday life.

*Are illusionist claiming that we are phenomenal zombies?* = Zombies are presented as having no inner life as Chalmers puts it "there is nothing it is like to be a zombie . . . all is dark inside". Illusionist will argue that inner life $\neq$ having phenomenal consciousness. For an illusionist, having an inner life "consists in having a form of introspective self-awareness that creates the illusion of a rich phenomenology" (p. 23). In that sense, zombies would have an inner life but we are imagining creatures with completely different introspective capacities. 

#### Why Illusionism?

A problem with conservative realism is that phenomenal properties seem to weird to yield to physical explanation. According to Frankish, 
> “most physicalists adopt a weakly illusionist view, denying that phenomenal properties are private, intrinsic, and ineffable and employing the phenomenal concept strategy to explain why they seem so. However, they insist that phenomenal properties are nonetheless real and genuinely qualitative . . .  If it is not to collapse into illusionism, then it must employ a notion of phenomenality that is stronger than that of quasi-phenomenality. Phenomenal properties must not merely cause representations of phenomenality but have some genuinely ‘feely’ aspect to them. And it is unclear what this could be. What phenomenal residue is left, once features such as privacy, intrinsicality, and ineffability have been stripped away? (Frankish, p. 26)”

One way to resist illusionist pressure, according to Frankish, is the accept the existence of an [[Levine-1983-the-explanatory-gap|explanatory-gap]] between phenomenal properties and their neural substrates.
