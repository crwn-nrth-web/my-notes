According to Quine, [[epistemological-gap-between-experience-and-theory|logical empiricism]] is a reductionist project with two aims 
- **conceptual side** = the aim was to clarify concepts or meanings involved in the sciences
	- in mathematics $\rightarrow$ reduce mathematical concepts to logic
	- in empirical science $\rightarrow$ reduce theoretical terms to observation statements
- **doctrinal side** = the aim of clarifying how we can know the truth of the claims made in the sciences
	- in mathematics $\rightarrow$ derive truth from self-evident logical principles
	- in empirical science $\rightarrow$ derive truth from observations

Quine's claim is that both sides fail, in both branches.
#### Defects in the mathematical branch
On the conceptual side, the reduction of mathematics to logical terms was not entirely successful. Instead of reducing it to pure logic alone, mathematic could only be reduced to logic and set theory.

This leads to a problem for the doctrinal side that was supposed to show that logic, and hence mathematics, is self-evident and certain. Set theory is not more obvious or certain that ordinary mathematics, and the reduction of mathematics to logic + set theory does not provide mathematics certainty. 

For Quine, if arithmetic is reduced to set theory and set theory is not more epistemically secure than arithmetic, then the reduction has no epistemic advantage.

> “This correction can be seen as a disappointment epistemologically, since the firmness and obviousness that we associate with logic cannot be claimed for set theory. But still the success achieved in the foundations of mathematics remains exemplary…”
#### Defects in the epistemological branch

>“The parallel is as follows. Just as mathematics is to be reduced to logic, or logic and set theory, so natural knowledge is to be based somehow on sense experience. This means explaining the notion of body in sensory terms; here is the conceptual side. And it means justifying our knowledge of truths of nature in sensory terms; here is the doctrinal side…”

Quine draws a parallel between mathematics and empirical science, and that the logical empiricist's attempt to reduce natural scientific theories to sense experience also fails. 

On the conceptual side, the translation of theoretical claims into claims about sense experience by [[Ayer-1936-definitions-in-use]] (contextual definitions) proved to be problematic. 

You cannot just talk about sense-data, you have to talk about "sets upon sets of sense data". He also notes that there is no real adequate way of reducing talk of spatial-temporal location to talk about sense-data, example = there is no satisfactory way to reduce "the body is over there" to pure sense-data talk.

On the doctrinal side, even if we could reduce the meanings of empirical claims to statements about sense-data, there still wouldn't be enough to establish how we can know the truth of the claims.

> “The mere fact that a sentence is couched in terms of observation — that is, in terms of sense-data — does not mean that it can be proved from observation sentences. The most modest of generalizations about observable traits will cover more cases than its utterer will have had occasion to observe.”

This idea is similar to the nature of generalizations made by [[Humean-theory-of-causation]] = they are universal and go beyond what we have observed.

Quine sees logical empiricism on failing against the challenges of [[Skepticism]].

> “The Cartesian quest for certainty had been the remote motivation for epistemology, both on its conceptual and doctrinal side, but that quest was a lost cause.”

If the reductionist program of logical empiricism fails, then how are we to understand the relevance of sensory experience to theory? Quine's answer to this is his idea of [[Quine-on-naturalized-epistemology|naturalized epistemology]].
