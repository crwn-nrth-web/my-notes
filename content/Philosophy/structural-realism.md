**Structural realism** = science describes structures of the world (not objects). Equations and models capture relations (structures) between objects.

This is in contrast to *entity realism* ([[Hacking-on-scientific-realism]]) that holds that science describes entities.
 
The motivation for this view is in response to [[pessimistic-meta-induction]] (our past successful theories turned out false, so how do we know our present successful theories are right). According to scientific structural realism, it is the structures that our theories get right, even if they're wrong about the nature of entities.
### Best of both worlds

> [!source|i:arrow-up-right-from-square c:a7c080]
> Worrall, John. 1989. “Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds?”. _Dialectica_ 43(1/2): 99–124. doi:[10.1111/j.1746-8361.1989.tb00933.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1746-8361.1989.tb00933.x).

**Core idea** 
- scientific structural realism is the *best of both worlds*: "to give the argument from scientific revolutions its full weight and yet still adopt some sort of realist attitude towards presently accepted theories in physics and elsewhere"
- Claim = what survives theory change. and what we can rationally believe, is the structure of our theories

John Worrall highlights two opposing arguments
1. [[no-miracles-argument]] = supports [[Scientific-realism]] that "our present theories are so empirically successful that they can't have gotten that way by chance and so must have latched onto the blueprint of the universe" (p. 99)
2. [[pessimistic-meta-induction]] = supports [[scientific-anti-realism]] that "there have been enormously successful theories which were once accepted but are now regarded as false". It asks what rational basis do we have for believing current theories describes the world, given that past successful theories were abandoned

The aim of structural realism is to satisfy both arguments.

##### How radical has theory change been?

[[Scientific-realism]] claims that we have grounds for holding our present theories to be approximately true

An important question for the realist is whether or not the history of science speaks in favor of successive scientific theories being increasingly good "approximations to the truth". Worral argues that this comes down to judgement on how radical theory change has been in science

Realism is plausible only if science has been "essentially cumulative", that deposed theories themselves (not just their successful empirical consequences) have lived on in some way.

Realism is incompatible with radical theoretical changes at the level of theoretical content ([[Kuhn-on-crisis-and-scientific-revolutions|scientific-revolution]]). If successor theories have nothing in common with their predecessors, except a broader but overlapping range of empirical consequences, then it is hard to say that there is an approximate truth that is captured by both stages of the theory.

##### If pessimistic met-induction holds
Worrall notes that if theory change is empirically cumulative but theoretically non-cumulative, we have two options = 

1. **constructive anti-realism** = Science is the realm of rationality, and it is irrational to accept claims that history suggests will probably be rejected. Since the theoretical claims have a poor track record and the empirical content is what survives, the rational attitude is to accept only the empirical content. Hence, scientific theories make no real claims beyond their observable consequences = [[Van-Fraassen-constructive-empiricism]]
2. **conjectural realism**= based on [[popper-1934-logic-of-scientific-discovery]]. We have reason to think that our presently best theories are our best shots at truth, since they withstand evidence better than their rivals. However, we have no reason to think they are approximately true, or that they are any closer to capturing the truth. 

Worrall argues instead for a third option **structural realism**.

Realism can be saved by finding continuity between successive theories, but at the level of structure (or mathematical formulation), instead of at the level of entities and causal powers. 

Examples:
- When optics shifted from Fresnel’s elastic solid ether theory to James Clerk Maxwell’s electromagnetic field theory, the physical belief in an "ether" was abandoned, but Fresnel’s mathematical equations for light propagation were preserved inside Maxwell's new framework. Since Fresnel's theory was empirically successful despite its false claim about the ether, its success must be credited to the structure captured in the equations, which is what survived.

#### main features of structural realism

In summary, main features of structural realism =
1. Scientific theories, at best, reveal the logical form or structure of the underlying physical reality by means of mathematical structure.
2. Mathematical equations which are retained in theory change express real relations between objects for which we know nothing more than they stand in these relations to one another, i.e. [[mathematical-structuralism]]

For Worrall, what is "essentially" correct about a current theory is not the constituents named by the current theory, as this can easily be under-determined by a new theory, but instead the *mathematical structure*.
