**Structural realism** = science describes structures of the world (not objects). Equations and models capture relations (structures) between objects.

This is in contrast to *entity realism* ([[Hacking-on-scientific-realism]]) that holds that science describes entities.

The motivation for this view is in response to [[pessimistic-meta-induction]] (our past successful theories turned out false, so how do we know our present successful theories are right). According to scientific structural realism, it is the structures that our theories get right, even if they're wrong about the nature of entities.
### Best of both worlds

> [!source|i:arrow-up-right-from-square c:a7c080]
> Worrall, John. 1989. “Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds?”. _Dialectica_ 43(1/2): 99–124. doi:[10.1111/j.1746-8361.1989.tb00933.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1746-8361.1989.tb00933.x).

John Worrall in his paper highlights 
- the main argument for [[Scientific-realism]] is that "our present theories in science are so successful empirically that they can't have gotten that way by chance - instead they must somehow have latched onto the blueprint of the universe" = [[no-miracles-argument]]
- the main argument for [[scientific-anti-realism]] is that "there have been enormously successful theories which were once accepted but are now regarded as false" = [[pessimistic-meta-induction]]

Both of these arguments are strong intuitive arguments: the [[no-miracles-argument]] can be seen as optimistic or wishful thinking by scientists that the scientific enterprise is flourishing, and it can be seen by the successfulness of our theories; we must have latched onto something in the blueprint of the universe . The [[pessimistic-meta-induction]] on the other hand offers a check on our intuitions to consider if our theories are actually tracking anything real.

Worrall argues that scientific structural realism is the *best of both worlds*: "to give the argument from scientific revolutions its full weight and yet still adopt some sort of realist attitude towards presently accepted theories in physics and elsewhere"

[[pessimistic-meta-induction]] asks what rational basis do we have for believing current theories describes the world, given that past successful theories were abandoned.

Worral argues that it comes down to judgement on how radical theory change has been in the history of science.
- If successor theories have nothing in common with their predecessors, except a broader but overlapping range of empirical consequences, then it is hard to say that there is an approximate truth that is captured by both stages of the theory. 
- But if we can argue that later theories typically build upon the (observational and theoretical) knowledge embodied in previous theories, then we can preserve 

Worrall argues for a more abstract way in which theories show continuity. Structural realism allows there to be continuity at the level of structure (or mathematical formulation), instead of at the level of entities and causal powers. 

Example: When optics shifted from Fresnel’s elastic solid ether theory to James Clerk Maxwell’s electromagnetic field theory, the physical belief in an "ether" was abandoned, but Fresnel’s mathematical equations for light propagation were preserved inside Maxwell's new framework. Since Fresnel's theory was empirically successful despite its false claim about the ether, its success must be credited to the structure captured in the equations, which is what survived.

In summary, main features of structural realism =
1. Scientific theories, at best, reveal the logical form or structure of the underlying physical reality by means of mathematical structure.
2. Mathematical equations which are retained in theory change express real relations between objects for which we know nothing more than they stand in these relations to one another, i.e. [[mathematical-structuralism]]

For Worrall, what is "essentially" correct about a current theory is not the constituents named by the current theory, as this can easily be under-determined by a new theory, but instead the *mathematical structure*.

[[Kuhn-on-crisis-and-scientific-revolutions]] is heavy on the discontinuity between scientific revolutions. Worrall is pushing against this discontinuity that he grants the history of radical change at the level of entities, but argues for continuity at the level of mathematical structure.
