> ***Prompt:* Is consciousness a problem for physicalist accounts of mind? Armstrong and Jackson arrive at very different positions on this question. What are their respective positions, and which of the two do you find more convincing? Briefly argue for the conclusion you arrive at.**

Physicalism claims that everything, including minds, can be explained in physical terms. The problem arises when it comes to explaining consciousness and our inner subjective experiences by purely physicalist accounts. Two contrasting approaches address the problem of consciousness: Armstrong, a reductive physicalist, claims it can be explained as perception of our inner states, while Jackson argues that Physicalism cannot capture the qualitative, subjective aspects of experience. This essay argues that while Armstrong provides a coherent and scientifically grounded model of consciousness as a physical process, Jackson’s Knowledge argument is more convincing because it reveals that a purely physical understanding of the mind leaves the subjective aspect of consciousness unexplained.

Armstrong adopts a strong type-physicalist explanation for minds, defining them as causes of certain behavior. He defines mental states as “a state of the person apt for producing certain ranges of behavior”. By this, he means that mental states are physical states that cause certain behavior. Since Physicalism is true, mental states are nothing more than states of the central nervous system. This view allows Armstrong to maintain a scientific account of the mind that identifies mental phenomena with purely physical processes. However, Armstrong acknowledges that traditional Physicalism struggles to explain first-person experience and so extends his account by offering a theory of consciousness as perception directed inwards.

For Armstrong, consciousness is the mind’s perception of its own states. Just as sense-perception allows us to act towards things outside of us, consciousness allows us to act towards what’s happening inside of us. Armstrong illustrates this through the example of automatic driving: when a person “comes to” after driving for a long time without awareness, it is clear that mental processes were guiding the car, but consciousness was absent. What was missing in such cases is not the physical processes themselves but the monitoring of those processes. Thus, consciousness, on Armstrong’s account, is a purely physical process in which brain monitors its own activity, giving us the capacity for selective behavior – a form of self-scanning within the nervous system.

While Armstrong’s model is coherent and aligns well with scientific naturalism, it faces significant challenges. By defining consciousness as self-monitoring, his view explains the function of being conscious, but not what it feels like to experience pain, color, emotion, etc. This leaves out the qualitative or phenomenal aspect, called qualia. Moreover, the type-identity claim underlying Armstrong’s theory is too restrictive; by linking mental states directly to neural states, it cannot account for multiple realizability. This is the idea that the same mental states can arise from different physical states in different kinds of beings. These limitations reveal that Armstrong’s reductionist model, while elegant, leaves the subjective essence of consciousness unexplained.

Frank Jackson’s Knowledge Argument offers one of the most influential challenges to Physicalism, aiming to show that conscious experience involves more than physical facts alone. Consider that Mary knows all the physical facts about the world but has lived her entire life in a black-and-white room and so has never experienced color, for instance. Jackson asserts that our intuition leads us to believe that Mary learns something new when she experiences color for the first time as she never knew what it looked like before. The new knowledge is not only knowledge about her own experience, but it’s also indirectly knowledge about other people’s experience. If Physicalism were true and complete, Mary’s physical knowledge should have included everything there is to know. Because she gains new knowledge, Jackson concludes that there are facts about consciousness – qualia – that are not physical facts. This suggest that Physicalism cannot capture the full reality of the mind.

The Knowledge argument effectively illustrates the explanatory gap between objective knowledge and subjective experience. It highlights that knowing all the physical details about brain processes still doesn’t convey what it is like to feel those processes from the inside. Thus, consciousness resists reduction to physical description because its subjective aspect cannot be captured in objective terms. A common objection claims that when Mary experience color for the first time, she doesn’t gain any new factual knowledge, juts new abilities to recognize or imagine color and therefore Physicalism could still be true. However, even these new abilities depends on new experiential knowledge which Physicalism cannot explain. The ability to recognize red presupposes knowing what red looks like, which is precisely the phenomenal aspect that Physicalism cannot account for. Hence, Jackson’s argument remains compelling despite such objections.

Between the two, Jackson’s argument more convincingly demonstrates why consciousness remains a genuine problem for physicalist theories. While Armstrong’s account is internally consistent and offers a scientifically grounded explanation of consciousness, it ultimately fails to explain why consciousness is accompanied by a qualitative aspect. Jackson’s Knowledge Argument reveals that even a complete physical description leaves something essential out, showing that Physicalism alone cannot bridge the gap between the physical and the phenomenal. Therefore, consciousness continues to challenge any account that seeks to reduce the mental to the physical.
