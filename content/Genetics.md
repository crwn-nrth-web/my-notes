# Examples of Artificial selection

Genetic traits are passed from one generation to the next, through **natural or artificial selection**.

Artificial selection = domestication

Purposeful matings of individuals with desirable phenotypes led to greater expression of those characteristics in offspring, resulting in **domestication** of plants and animals.

**Example of domestication** 
- first successful domestication was wolves to dogs 15,000 years ago
- dogs and wolves share the same genes, but have different alleles of those genes
- what determines that they're now considered different species, is that left to their own devices, wolves and dogs do not intermingle. In other words, they are different species because the behavior of the two groups are different

#### Belyaev's foxes
Dimitri Belyaev in Stalin Russia during the 1950s began an experiment to breed silver foxes that were easier to handle i.e prosocial towards humans. 

Within six generations of selective breeding, Belyaev obtained domesticated foxes, that were friendly to humans, but also contained other characteristics (phenotypes) including floppy ears, short and curly tails, reduced stress hormones, mottled fur patterns, juvenilized facial features etc. Despite selecting only based on behavior, the foxes obtained these other traits, known as domestication syndrome. This suggests that traits in the domestication syndrome are genetically linked to genes associated with prosocial-towards-human behavior.

He also conducting another experiment using a hyper-aggressive line of foxes. It was seen that genetically aggressive foxes raised by domesticated human-happy foxes remained human-aggressive, while human-friendly foxes raised by a human-aggressive mother remained human friendly. This suggests that genes have a profound influence not just on the phenotype but with behavioral characteristics. 

# Mendelian Genetics

**phenotype** = observable characteristics (physical or behavioral) largely determined by genotype, and commonly referred to as a trait

**genotype** = genetic makeup; description of the genetic information carried by an individual

Mendel developed theories of inheritance through breeding experiments with garden peas.

- they were easy to cross-fertilize, had large number of offsprings and a short growing season
- there were clear cut alternative forms of particular phenotypes = white vs purple flowers, yellow vs green peas, round vs wrinkled peas
- there were true breeding lines = offsprings carrying parental traits remained constant from generation to generation
	- to obtain true breeding lines, he would let the plants with the desired phenotype to self-fertilize

- In order to control his crosses, he would prevent self-fertilization by removing the anthers (pollen producing parts) before they were matured from the plan he wanted to cross to.
- Tall vs short plants = reciprocal breeding meant taking the pollen (male characteristics) of the dwarf plant and transferring it to the tall plant or vice versa (short female, tall male)

Note that in garden peas, each seed is an individual embryo with different phenotype.
