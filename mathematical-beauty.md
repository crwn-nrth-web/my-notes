## Dickson, Beauty Doth of Itself Persuade

> [!source]
> Dickson, M. (2010) *“Beauty Doth of Itself Persuade: Dirac on Quantization, Mathematical Beauty, and Theoretical Understanding,”* in Discourse on a New Method:  Reinvigorating the Marriage of History and Philosophy of Science

Dickson argues in this paper that mathematical beauty is connected, in some way, with out theoretical understanding of the world.

This paper is focusing on beauty as this objective criteria, attempting to give it an objection definition. An argument can be made that Dirac and Dickson are pulling this definition from Platonism: beauty as a Form, in turn as a part of objective reasoning (beauty is where the numbers are)

##### Dirac on mathematical beauty: simplicity and generality

Dirac insisted that less than beautiful theories are *ispo facto* i.e. somehow incomplete or need of revision.

> “As time goes on, it becomes increasingly evident that the rules which the mathematician finds interesting are the same as those which Nature has chosen.” (Dirac, 1939)

While mathematical beauty encompasses many qualities, Dirac places emphasis on: simplicity and generality.
- Dirac defines **mathematical simplicity** (in a rough sense) to be "the minimization of the number of boundary conditions” (Dickson, p. 406) 

How does this contribute to understanding? Increasing simplicity (by Dirac's definition) increases the mathematical part of a theory. Boundary conditions are given by the world, not a theory, so removing them makes the theory more mathematical. 

Increasing generality, in turn, also increases understanding, according to Dickson. 

**Generality** = a theory that is more general encompasses more phenomena/observations/explanations. A more general theory $T*$ also allows us to understand theory $T$ as a more specific case. For this paper, generality = understanding of a theory, rather than using theory to understand the world.


> "Suppose that we begin with some theory, T, that describes some phenomena, P. Theoretical work produces a new theory, $T*$, which is more general than T. In virtue of $T*$, we have a new kind of understanding of P, according to which P comes under not only concepts from T but also concepts from $T*$. More important, however, we now have an understanding of T itself, in virtue of seeing it as a special case of the more general theory, $T*$. In this paper, I am more interested in theoretical understanding in this latter sense (==🟠‘understanding of a theory’ as opposed to ‘using theory to understand some phenomenon’==)." (p. 409)

Dickson's main argument is based on an example: quantization of quantum mechanics (analysis of the relationship between classical mechanics and quantum mechanics), where he makes the following conclusion:

> “the more general argument is more beautiful, i.e., more general. And beauty, as I claimed above, generates understanding: In this case, it allows one to see quantum theory as an example of a more general class of theories” (Dickson, p. 413)

However, he notes, in the paper, that the project of quantization was incorrect. This conclusion that "beauty / generality generates understanding" can be seen *if* Dirac's quantization worked. This project was important for Dirac as it remained in his textbook despite major revisions elsewhere. 

In fact, quantization of quantum mechanics is still an active research project, to find a general theory that would connect classical mechanics to quantum mechanics rather than a case-by-case application. 

### unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics and beauty

[[Wigner-and-Islami-on-the-unreasonable-effectiveness-of-mathematics-in-physics|wigner-on-the-unreasonable-effectiveness-of-mathematics-in-physics]] wonders "why should the world care about mathematical beauty?" 

A version of this from [[Steiner-on-the-applicability-of-mathematics]] (1998) [^2] =
1. Mathematical concepts arise from the aesthetic impulse in humans.
2. It is unreasonable to expect that what arises from the aesthetic impulse in humans should be significantly effective in physics.
3. Nevertheless, a significant number of these concepts are significantly effective in physics.  
Hence, mathematical concepts are unreasonably effective in physics

So there are two mysteries:
- why should mathematics be applicable in the first place / why should nature be mathematized?
- granted that mathematics is applicable, why should mathematical beauty be a guide to how to pursue applied mathematics / why should nature care about beauty?

## Hossenfelder, How beauty leads physics astray

> [!source]
> Hossenfelder, S. (2018). *Lost in math: How beauty leads physics astray.* Chapter 1 - 2 (p. 1 -  41). Basic Books

Hossenfelder focuses on the biases scientists hold when it comes to aesthetic appeal and beauty, and the way that these biases can be detrimental to the progress in modern theoretical physics.

> "The invention of new natural laws -- theory development -- is not taught in class and not explained in textbooks. Some of it physicists learn studying the history of science, but most of it they pick up from older colleagues, friends and mentors, supervisors and reviewers. Handed from one generation to the next, much of it is experience, a hard-earned intuition for what works. When asked to judge the promise of a newly invented but untested theory, physicists draw upon the concepts of naturalness, simplicity or elegance, and beauty. These hidden rules are ubiquitous in the foundations of physics. They are invaluable. And in utter conflict with the scientific mandate of objectivity."

Hossenfelder notes that scientists talk about the "importance of unbiased empirical judgement and on the other hand use aesthetic criteria to defend their favorite theories"

The standard model, developed through the latter part of the 20th century, is the most precise and successful big theory of anything that has been created and it is at the heart of theoretical physics. The model incorporates 25 fundamentally irreducible "particles" and fields that support them and their interactions. However, there is a great deal of dissatisfaction, confusion, and downright discouragement within the field, mainly because of its "ugliness". Theoretical particle physicists find the standard model ugly because many of the detailed properties of nature that it predicts do not always emerge gracefully and orignaically from prior laws or principles of nature. Their properties are fine-tuned, not natural. String theory and supersymmetry are two prominent candidates proposed to eliminate the perceived ugliness.

**Scientific theories are mathematical** =

> " . . .  nature plays by the rules of mathematics. In physics, theories are mode of math . . .  Math keeps us honest -- it prevents us from lying to ourselves and to each other. You can be wrong with math, but you can't lie. Our task as theoretical physicists is to develop the mathematics to either describe existing observations, or to make predictions that guide experimental strategies. Using mathematics in theory development enforces logical rigor and internal consistency"

Besides using mathematics to "keep us honest", mathematics is the most economical and unambiguous language that we have.

**relationship between physicists and mathematical physicists** =

> "In physics, theories almost always start out as loose patchworks of ideas. Cleaning up the mess that physicists generate in theory development, and finding a neat set of assumptions from which the whole theory can be derived, is often left to our col-leagues in mathematical physics-a branch of mathematics, not of physics.
> For the most part, physicists and mathematicians have settled on a fine division of labor in which the former complain about the finicki-ness of the latter, and the latter complain about the sloppiness of the former. On both sides, though, we are crucially aware that progress in one field drives progress in the other."


> "Demonstrating that a new theory reproduces all the achievements of successful old theories can be extremely difficult. This is because a new theory might use an entirely different mathematical framework that looks nothing like that of the old theory. Finding a way to show that both nevertheless arrive at the same predictions for already-made observations often requires finding a suitable way to reformulate the new theory. This is straightforward in cases where the new theory directly employs the math of the old one, but it can be a big hurdle with entirely new frameworks."

Echoing an idea talked about in [[Wigner-and-Islami-on-the-unreasonable-effectiveness-of-mathematics-in-physics|Islami-interpretation-of-wigner]], mathematics works so successfully in physics because the subjects are highly reproducible. Using mathematics, "a language so rigid", doesn't make sense if the data doesn't match the rigor. Results in psychology are hard to reproduce because no two people are alike, a problem physics doesn't have.

**Why scientists are drawn to aesthetic appeal?**

As our theories get more and more developed, it becomes harder to propose a new theory/improvement. The requirement of a new theory is that it needs to be consistent, both internally and consistent with experiment. So the more observations our theories could describe, the more difficult it became to test a proposed improvement, as a new theory would need to accommodate all of the present theories' success and still be a little better, which takes a lot of time, perhaps longer than a scientist's full career. 


> "This forces theorists to draw upon criteria other than empirical adequacy to decide which research avenues to pursue. Aesthetic appeal is one of them"

An example of aesthetic appeal is *supersymmetry* (SuSy). Supersymmetry fits so snugly with our existing theories that many physicists are convinced that it must be right, despite the fact that none of the predicted super-particles have been observed or detected. "The ideas behind supersymmetry are simply too beautiful and too elegant not to be part of the universe" (p. 9) [^1]

Another appeal to supersymmetry is that its more natural (by avoiding fine-tuning), but if its right, then we should see new particles. 

The more removed from experimental test a subject area is (foundational physics), the more influence aesthetic appeal has on its theories.

**measuring naturalness** 

Gian pioneered a method to quantify how "natural" a theory is, a mathematical measure from which one can read off how much a theory relies on improbable coincidences. The more natural a theory, the less coincidences it requires, and the more appealing it is. (p. 4)

A widespread belief that the mathematical formulae to measure naturalness rests on is that a theory with very large or very small numbers isn't pretty. And so, physicists become more convinced that in order to avoid ugly  numerical coincidences new discoveries had to be made.

Physicists calls a number that seems to require explanation = fine-tuned, while a theory that has no fine-tuned numbers is "natural"

A natural theory is also often described as one that uses only numbers close to 1 (p. 38)

**historical account of the role of beauty in science**
- until the end of the nineteenth century, it was quite common for scientists to view nature's beauty as a sing of divinity
	- e.g. Kepler originally proposed a model of the solar system with the radii of the orbits determined by platonic solids. When he later corrected that planets move in ellipses, not circles, it was met with disapproval as it didn't match the aesthetic appeal of the time. 
- as science separated from religion and became more professionalized, beauty stopped being equated to godly influence
- in the 20th century, aesthetic appeal morphed from a bonus of scientific theories to a guide in their development, until aesthetic principles turned to mathematical naturalness

> "One only has to look through the back files of scientific periodicals, say of 50 years back, to come across dozens of papers which have served no purpose in the extension of scientific knowledge and which never could have done, mere fungi on the trunk of the scientific tree, and like fungi, constantly reappearing if swept away.... [But if a paper] ==🟠evokes in us those emotions which we associate with beauty no further justification is needed==; it is not a fungus but a blossom; it is a terminus of science, the end of a line of inquiry in which science has reached its ultimate goal. It is the ugly papers which require justification. (Edward Arthur Milne, 1922 talk at the Cambridge University Natural Science club)

> "If nature leads us to mathematical forms of great simplicity and beauty we cannot help thinking that they are 'true', that they reveal a genuine feature of nature" (Heisenberg)

It's important for scientists to recognize that the beauty of a theory is not a guarantee that it will be correct. 
- Vortex theory (explaining the variety of atoms by knots of different types) became popular because of its beauty, but it was proven to be wrong with the advent of quantum mechanics. 
- Einstein rejected Lemaitre's Big Bang theory because he found it "abominable". Einstein instead introduced a cosmological constant to force the universe into static configuration, which turned out to be incorrect. And the Big Bang theory is now considered correct despite its non-aesthetic appeal

And "ugly" ideas have turned out to be correct, like electrodynamics and quantum electrodynamics.

**concern about the role of beauty in physics**
Hossenfelder notes that theory assessment (deciding what theories are worth testing) depends on criteria other than empirical adequacy (it has to cause testing all possible hypotheses is simply infeasible) and these criteria are often social and aesthetic.

Nature is not obliged to conform to our biases of aesthetic appeal.

The problem with relying on beauty as a criteria is that testing a theory takes decades and billions of dollars to carry out. "The more difficult new experiments become, the more care scientists must take to not sleepwalk into a dead end while caught up in a beautiful dream" (p. 35)

Hossenfelder makes a comment on [[popper-1934-logic-of-scientific-discovery|popper-falsification-of-a-theory]] that it is rarely possible to falsify a theory, since ideas can be modified or extended to match incoming evidence. Instead, we should *implausify* them
